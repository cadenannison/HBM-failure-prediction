## Slide 4
> Notes: Faithfulness: Is the Answer Supported by Its Context?  OpeningWe’ve used the word “faithfulness” on the evaluation gate, the monitoring dashboard, and the incident playbook. This slide slows down and defines it, because it’s the metric students most often misunderstand. Read the definition box: faithfulness measures whether the factual claims in an answer are supported by the retrieved context. Then the bold line: judge the answer against the evidence the model received. Not against the truth, not against what you know, but against what was in the context window.  Left panel: the retrieved contextThree facts came back from retrieval: the customer bought running shoes, the current offer is $5 off socks, and the offer expires October 20. That is the model’s entire world for this question.  Right panels: two answers The faithful answer makes three factual claims: purchased running shoes, $5 off socks, through October 20. Each one matches a line in the context. Three of three supported, so a faithfulness score of 1.0. The partly unfaithful answer is identical except for one number: $10 instead of $5. The purchase and the expiry are still supported; the discount isn’t. Two of three claims supported, about 0.67. Point out how small the difference is. One digit. The sentence is fluent, confident, and mostly right. That’s what makes hallucinations dangerous: they hide inside otherwise correct answers. Then connect it to the gate: 0.67 is far below our 0.95 threshold, so this answer would fail.  The formulaFaithfulness equals supported factual claims divided by total factual claims. Note the second line: automated evaluators like RAGAS break answers into claims their own way. One evaluator might count “you qualify for $5 off socks through October 20” as one claim, another as two. Scores from different evaluators, or different versions of one evaluator, aren’t directly comparable. That’s why we pin evaluation versions.  The callout: faithful is not the same as correctThis is the most important part of the slide, and it’s Activity 1 again. Suppose a stale document in the index says $10, and the answer says $10. The answer is perfectly faithful: it matches its context exactly. But the live promotion is $5, so the customer gets the wrong offer. Ask the class to compare the two $10s on this slide. In the yellow panel, $10 is unfaithful, because the context said $5. In the callout, $10 is faithful, because the context said $10. Same answer, opposite score. Faithfulness is always relative to the context, so it can only be as good as the context is. Then the last line: check freshness and source accuracy separately, and a citation alone is not proof of support. A citation shows where the model says a claim came from. It doesn’t prove the source actually says that, and it doesn’t prove the source is current.  Tie-backThree checks, three different questions: Faithfulness: did the model stick to its evidence? Evaluation gate, sampled monitoring. Freshness: is the evidence current? Index operations and sync monitoring. Source accuracy: is the evidence right in the first place? Data ownership upstream. No single metric covers all three.  TransitionLead into the next metric (answer relevancy or context recall), or back to the evaluation gate, depending on where this slide sits in the deck.

## Slide 5
> Notes: Answer Relevancy: Does the Answer Address the Question?  OpeningThe last slide asked whether an answer sticks to its evidence. This one asks a different question: does the answer actually address what the customer asked? Read the bold line in the definition box: judge the answer against what the user asked. Faithfulness looks at the context; relevancy looks at the question.  Left panel: the question and the contextThe customer asks two things in one sentence: which sock discount they can use, and when it expires. Point out that it’s a two-part question, because that’s what makes the “partly relevant” example possible. The retrieved context has three facts: the $5 offer, the October 20 expiry, and store hours. Note that store hours is irrelevant to the question but was still retrieved. Retrieval often brings back extra material, and the model has to choose what to use.  Right panels: three answers Highly relevant: “You can use $5 off socks through October 20.” Both parts of the question answered, directly, with nothing extra. Partly relevant: “You can use $5 off socks.” Correct and faithful, but it answers only half the question. The customer still doesn’t know when the offer ends. Incomplete answers are a quiet failure, because nothing in them is wrong. Faithful, but irrelevant: “The store is open from 9 a.m. to 9 p.m.” Every word fits the context, so it would score perfectly on faithfulness, but it doesn’t answer the question. This example is why we need both metrics.  The check bandThree things a relevancy evaluator looks for: directness (does it get to the point?), coverage (does it answer every part of the question?), and unnecessary or off-topic content (does it pad the answer?).  For the technically curious, explain how RAGAS scores this. It doesn’t compare the answer to a reference answer. Instead, an LLM generates several questions the answer would be a good response to, and the evaluator measures how similar those questions are to the customer’s actual question. “The store is open 9 to 9” would produce questions about store hours, far from a question about sock discounts, so it scores low. An answer that covers only half the question generates questions that only partly match.  The second line matters: scores depend on the evaluator and its settings, and these examples are qualitative. As with faithfulness, pin the evaluator version.  The callout: relevant is not the same as faithful“$10 off socks through October 20” answers both parts of the question directly, so it would score high on relevancy. But it invents the discount amount. A relevancy evaluator doesn’t check facts against the context, so it can’t catch this. Put the two slides together as a simple grid on the whiteboard: Faithful and relevant: “$5 off socks through October 20.” The goal. Faithful but irrelevant: “The store is open 9 to 9.” Relevant but unfaithful: “$10 off socks through October 20.” Neither: an off-topic hallucination. Each metric catches what the other misses, which is why the evaluation gate requires both: faithfulness ≥ 0.95 and answer relevancy ≥ 0.85.  TransitionWe’ve now checked the answer against its evidence and against the question. Next: did retrieval bring back the right evidence in the first place? That’s context recall.  Note:  Context precision judges retrieval. It asks: of the chunks the retriever returned, how many are actually useful for answering the question, and are the useful ones ranked near the top? It scores the evidence, before the model writes anything. Answer relevancy judges generation. It asks: does the final answer address what the customer asked, completely and without padding? It scores the answer, regardless of what was retrieved.  With the NorthStar example: Retrieval returns “$5 off socks” and “Store hours: 9 a.m.–9 p.m.” Precision drops because half the evidence is noise. If store hours ranks first, precision drops further because RAGAS precision is rank-aware. The model ignores the store hours and answers “You can use $5 off socks.” That answer is fully on-topic for the discount part. So precision is mediocre while relevancy is decent: the model compensated for noisy retrieval. Flip it: retrieval returns only the two right chunks (precision 1.0), but the model answers “Thanks for shopping with NorthStar! We have many great offers…” Precision is perfect while relevancy is low: good evidence, bad answer.  How they’re computed in RAGAS: Context precision: an LLM judges each retrieved chunk as useful or not for answering the question, using the reference answer, or the generated answer in the reference-free variant. Useful chunks ranked higher score better. Answer relevancy: an LLM generates several questions the answer would be a good response to, and the evaluator measures how similar those are to the original question. The retrieved context isn’t used at all.  A teaching shortcut: recall and precision are a pair for retrieval (did we find what we needed, and how much junk came with it?). Faithfulness and relevancy are a pair for generation (did the answer stick to the evidence, and did it address the question?). Noisy retrieval doesn’t always show up in the answer, but it costs you. Extra chunks add tokens on every call, and the more irrelevant text in the context, the more chances the model has to latch onto the wrong fact.

## Slide 6
> Notes: Context Recall: Did Retrieval Find the Evidence We Need?  OpeningThe last two metrics judged the answer: against its evidence (faithfulness) and against the question (relevancy). Context recall steps back one stage and judges retrieval. Before the model writes anything, did the retriever bring back the facts needed to answer? Read the bold line in the definition box: judge the retrieved evidence against a known reference answer. That phrase “known reference answer” is the key difference from the other two metrics, and we’ll come back to it.  Left panel: the evaluation caseThis is one entry from the evaluation set: a question, a reference answer written by a person, and the facts that answer depends on. The same question as the relevancy slide, with two required facts: the $5 discount and the October 20 expiry. Context recall asks how many of those facts appear in what retrieval returned.  Right panels: two retrievals Complete retrieval: both chunks came back, the offer and the expiry. Both reference facts are supported: 2 of 2, a recall of 1.0. Incomplete retrieval: the offer came back, but instead of the expiry chunk, retrieval returned store hours. The discount is supported, the expiry isn’t: 1 of 2, a recall of 0.5. Point out that store hours didn’t hurt recall directly. Recall only asks whether the needed facts are present; it doesn’t penalize extra material. The problem is that the irrelevant chunk took the slot the expiry chunk needed. Measuring noise is a separate metric, context precision.  The formulaContext recall equals supported reference claims divided by total reference claims. Same caution as before: evaluators split claims differently, so pin the evaluator version.  The callout: missing evidence is an answer-quality riskWith the incomplete context, the model has two choices, and both are bad: It sticks to the evidence and says “You can use $5 off socks.” That’s the partly relevant answer from the last slide: faithful, but incomplete. It fills the gap with a guessed expiry date. Now the answer is complete but unfaithful. Either way, the root cause isn’t the model. It’s retrieval. That’s the point of the last line: recall checks evidence coverage, faithfulness checks whether the answer stays grounded. A low faithfulness or relevancy score can be a retrieval problem in disguise.  Why it needs a reference answerReturn to that key phrase. You can score faithfulness and relevancy on any live response because they only need the question, context, and answer. Context recall needs to know what the right answer should contain, so it only works on a labeled evaluation set. That’s why it appears in the evaluation gate (≥ 0.80) but not on the live monitoring dashboard.  Where it points in an incidentIf context recall drops after a change, look at the index, not the prompt: chunking, the embedding model, how many chunks are retrieved, or missing documents. Recall the incident playbook: “find what changed.” A recall drop narrows that search to retrieval.  Tie-togetherThe three metrics form a chain: Context recall: did retrieval find the evidence? Faithfulness: did the answer stick to the evidence? Answer relevancy: did the answer address the question? A failure at any link produces a bad answer, and each metric tells you which link broke. ## Slide 8
> Notes: Format Compliance: Does the Output Match the Contract?  OpeningThe last three metrics needed an LLM judge: faithfulness, relevancy, and context recall. Format compliance is different. Read the bold line: judge the output against an explicit, machine-checkable contract. No judge, no sampling, no evaluator version to pin. Plain code checks it, so it’s cheap enough to run on every response.  Why format matters at allThe answer isn’t read only by a customer. Downstream code parses it: it splits each line on the pipes, reads the amount as a number, and stores the expiry as a date. If the format breaks, the code breaks, so a format failure is a production bug, not a style issue.  Left panel: the contractThis is the same format line from the prompt template we versioned earlier: offer type, amount, expiry, reason, separated by pipes. The rules make it checkable: exactly three offer lines exactly four pipe-separated fields per line an amount that’s a dollar sign plus a number an expiry in YYYY-MM-DD format, and a reason that isn’t empty no headings or extra prose Point out that every rule is something a few lines of Python or a regular expression can check. That’s the test of a good contract: if you can’t write code to check a rule, it doesn’t belong in the format contract.  Right panels: pass and fail PASS: three lines, four fields each, valid amounts, valid dates. A parser handles it without any special cases. FAIL, useful prose, wrong format: “Save $5 on socks through October 20 because you recently bought shoes.” A customer would find this perfectly helpful. It’s even faithful and relevant. But it fails the contract three ways: one offer instead of three, no pipe-separated fields, and the date isn’t YYYY-MM-DD. Make the point that a good answer in the wrong format still breaks the system.  The formula bandFormat compliance rate equals responses that pass all format checks, divided by responses tested. A response with one bad field fails as a whole, because one bad field is enough to break the parser. Walk through the example: 49 of 50 pass, or 98%. That sounds excellent, and it fails the release gate, because the gate is 100%. Recall the evaluation gate slide: format is the one metric that has to be perfect. Then the last line: don’t average field-level pass rates. If you scored each field separately, that one bad response might be 95% correct field by field, and the average across all fields would look like 99.9%. That hides a response that would crash the parser. Count responses, not fields.  The callout: correct format isn’t correct content“Socks | $10 | 2026-10-20 | Recent shoe purchase” passes every format check. It’s also the invented discount from the faithfulness slide. The format validator can’t know $10 is wrong; it only knows $10 is a dollar sign followed by a number. Likewise, a correctly formatted expiry date can already be in the past: that’s a freshness problem, not a format problem.  So the division of labor is: deterministic code for structure, LLM-judged metrics for faithfulness and relevancy, and index operations for freshness.  In productionBecause the check is deterministic and cheap, it runs on 100% of live traffic (see the monitoring slide). When a response fails, the application shouldn’t pass it downstream. Common options are to retry once, ask the model to repair the output, or fall back to a safe default. Many teams also reduce format failures at the source by asking the model for structured output, for example JSON validated against a schema, instead of free text. > Notes: KEY MESSAGE: A prompt template is a production artifact. It lives in Git, has a version number, and can't be promoted without passing tests.  Prompt Templates Are Production Artifacts Opening (the big idea)So far, we've treated model weights, feature pipelines, and endpoints as things we version, test, and deploy carefully. In an LLM system, the prompt deserves the same treatment. The prompt is application logic. Change one sentence, and you can change output format, tone, hallucination rate, and cost per call, with no code change and no model retraining. If it can break production, it's a production artifact.  Left side: anatomy of the template for the Offer GenerationWalk through the YAML from top to bottom. The file name and metadata. The version lives in both the filename and the version field, along with a created date and an owning team. When something goes wrong in production, the first question is "which prompt was live?" This metadata answers it, and it should also be logged with every inference call. The system prompt. It sets the role (a retail marketing specialist for NorthStar) and then a RULES block. Point out what each rule does. "Ground offers in retrieved customer history" and "never fabricate purchase history" are guardrails against hallucination. "Include a specific discount amount" and the pipe-delimited format make the output machine-parseable, so downstream code can consume it. The INSUFFICIENT_DATA rule gives the model a safe way out instead of guessing when there's no data. The last rule, "cite the purchase or offer that supports each reason," is the newest one, and it turns the grounding rules from a request into something we can check. If every reason has to name a specific purchase or prior offer, we can verify that citation against the data we actually sent in. The user template. The placeholders in curly braces are filled at runtime: segment, 90-day purchase history, browse history, and retrieved_context, which comes from retrieval of similar accepted offers. The template is constant, and the data varies. That separation is what makes the prompt testable. The test cases. Twenty format and content tests live in the same file as the prompt. The prompt and its tests travel together, just like code and unit tests.  Right side, top: versioned in GitRead the history as a story. v3.0 added the RULES block. v3.1 added INSUFFICIENT_DATA handling, probably because the model was inventing offers for customers with thin history. v3.2 added the citation requirement, and you can see it as the last rule in the YAML on the left. It’s what’s on main and in production. Notice that each version maps to a visible change in the file, which is exactly what a reviewer sees in the Git diff. Meanwhile, an experimental branch, offer-v4-concise, is running as an A/B test. Each version is a small, explainable change tied to an observed problem. That's the discipline we want.  Right side, bottom: test before productionFour practices: Every prompt change gets a version bump. No silent edits, including "just fixing a typo." Store prompts in Git, not S3. Students will ask about this because our labs use S3 heavily for artifacts. S3 versioning keeps old copies, but Git gives you diffs, pull-request review, branches, and blame. A prompt change should get the same code review as a code change. S3 can still be the deployment target that CI publishes to, but Git is the source of truth. Run the test suite on every version. Format compliance is cheap to check automatically: does the output parse into four pipe-delimited fields? Content checks such as "no fabricated purchases" can use reference cases or an LLM-as-judge evaluator. A/B test major changes. Offline tests catch regressions. Only live traffic tells you whether "concise" offers actually get accepted more often. ## Slide 11
> Notes: KEY MESSAGE: The LLMOps release gate is an automated evaluation suite. Any prompt or retrieval change runs 50 test cases, gets scored, and is either promoted or blocked.  FLOW: Change, then generate answers for 50 cases, then score with RAGAS plus deterministic checks.  METRICS: - Faithfulness at least 0.95: are the claims supported by the retrieved context? The same 0.95 grounding test from the previous slide. - Answer relevancy at least 0.85: does it address the question? Newer RAGAS versions call this "response relevancy". - Context recall at least 0.80: did retrieval find what was needed? This needs reference answers, so each of the 50 cases needs a ground truth someone wrote. - Format compliance exactly 1.00: deterministic, no LLM judge needed.  DECISION: All pass, promote. Any fail, block and notify the owner.  GATE VS ALERT: The pre-release gate is 0.95, but the production alert we'll see on the incident-response slide fires at 0.90. Offline gates are stricter because they run on a curated set; production scores from 5% sampling are noisier, so the alert threshold leaves room.  CAVEATS: RAGAS metrics use an LLM as judge, so pin the package version, the judge model and the settings, or scores drift between runs. With only 50 cases, a 0.01 to 0.02 difference is within noise. Scores are evidence, not guarantees. Compare against the baseline and read the failures.  Reference: docs.ragas.io for available metrics.  TRANSITION: Prompts are the first artifact. The retrieval index is the second.  LLMOps Evaluation: Automated Quality Gates  Opening (pick up from the last slide) This slide answers the obvious next question: how do we decide a new version is good enough to ship? The answer is the same one we use for models: an automated gate in the pipeline that either promotes the release or blocks it, with no one eyeballing outputs and saying "looks fine."  Top row: the pipelineWalk left to right. Prompt / index change. Two things trigger the pipeline. A prompt change is what we just saw, like v3.1 to v3.2. An index change is easy to overlook: if you re-chunk documents, swap the embedding model, or reload the vector store, the prompt didn't change, but the answers can. Both get evaluated. Generate answers on 50 test cases. Run the candidate version against a fixed evaluation set of questions with known good reference answers. Fixed is the key word: if the test set changes between runs, the scores aren't comparable. Score quality with RAGAS plus checks. RAGAS is an open-source framework for scoring retrieval-augmented generation. It uses an LLM as the judge for most metrics. "Checks" are deterministic tests, plain code with no LLM involved.  The table: four metrics, four gatesExplain what each metric asks, in plain language. Faithfulness, gate ≥ 0.95. Is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved context? This is our hallucination metric, and that's why it has the strictest LLM-judged threshold. For the NorthStar offer generator, a low faithfulness score means the model is inventing purchases. Answer relevancy, gate ≥ 0.85. Did the answer actually address the question? A response can be perfectly faithful and still be off-topic. Context recall, gate ≥ 0.80. Did retrieval find the information needed to answer? This one measures the retriever, not the generator. If it drops after an index change, the problem is chunking or embeddings, not the prompt. Format compliance, gate = 1.00. This is a deterministic check, so it has to be perfect. Downstream code parses the pipe-delimited output; one malformed response is a production bug. This is also where the citation rule from v3.2 gets enforced: the check can confirm every reason cites something. Point out that the thresholds aren't arbitrary. They reflect cost of failure. A hallucinated offer is worse than a slightly off-topic one, so faithfulness gets the higher bar.  Right side: the decisionThe logic is an AND: all metrics must pass to promote. Any single failure blocks the release and notifies the owner (the author field from the prompt YAML). There's no averaging across metrics, because a great relevancy score can't make up for hallucinations.  Bottom banner: three practices that make the gate trustworthy Pin evaluation versions. Pin the test set, the RAGAS version, and the judge model with its settings. If the judge model gets upgraded, scores can shift even though your system didn't change. Without pinning, you can't tell whether your prompt got worse or your ruler moved. Compare with the baseline. The thresholds are a floor. Also compare with the current production version. If production scores 0.99 faithfulness and the candidate scores 0.96, it passes the gate but it's a regression, and someone should look at it before release. Review failures before release. Scores tell you how much, but reading the failed cases tells you why. With 50 cases, that's a few minutes of reading, and it's often where you find the real bug.  A caution worth saying aloudLLM-judged scores are noisy. On 50 cases, a run-to-run swing of a point or two is normal. That's another reason to pin the judge and compare against a baseline rather than treating 0.95 as a hard physical constant.  Tie-back to the labsThis is the same pattern as the model evaluation gate in our MLOps pipeline: evaluate the candidate, compare against thresholds and the current champion, and promote only on pass. The artifact under test is a prompt and an index instead of a model, but the pipeline shape is identical.  TransitionThe gate protects us before release. ## Slide 12
> Notes: OpeningWe've built a RAG pipeline. How do we know if it's any good, and when it isn't, how do we know what to fix? RAGAS, short for Retrieval-Augmented Generation Assessment, is an open-source framework built for exactly that question.  Why RAG is harder to evaluateA normal LLM app has one moving part. A RAG app has at least two: a retriever that finds evidence and an LLM that writes an answer from it. When the answer is wrong, there are four possible culprits: the retriever fetched the wrong documents, it fetched the right ones but missed something, the LLM ignored the evidence, or the LLM made something up. A single accuracy score can't tell those apart. RAGAS scores each stage separately.  The exampleTake a simple question: "What year was SageMaker introduced?" Our system retrieves some passages and answers "re:Invent, 2017." That's correct, but we still want to know whether it got there for the right reasons.  Retrieval metrics (blue) Context precision asks whether the retrieved chunks are relevant and whether the useful ones are ranked near the top. Low precision means noise: the LLM has to dig through junk. Context recall asks whether retrieval found all the evidence needed. It compares what we retrieved against a reference answer, so this is the one metric that needs ground truth. Low recall means gaps: the answer can't be complete because the evidence never arrived.  Generation metrics (purple) Faithfulness breaks the answer into individual claims and checks each one against the retrieved context. If the answer says 2017 but the context never mentions a year, that's a hallucination, even if 2017 happens to be right. Answer relevancy asks whether the answer actually addresses the question. One caution: it measures relevance, not correctness. A confident, on-topic, wrong answer can still score well, which is why we look at all four together.  The diagnosis (bottom left)Here's where RAGAS pays off. Say our system gets 82% of questions right, and we want to know why the other 18% fail. Precision is 0.91, so what we retrieve is mostly relevant. Faithfulness is 0.95, so the LLM sticks to the evidence. Relevancy is 0.89, so the answers stay on topic. Recall is 0.62, so we're missing evidence.  The verdict is clear: this is a retrieval problem. Swapping in a bigger LLM would spend money on the part that already works. Instead, we'd look at chunking, top_k, hybrid search, reranking, or gaps in the knowledge base itself. That's the core idea: evaluation becomes a diagnosis, not just a grade.  LLM as judge (bottom right)Who scores all these claims? Usually, another LLM acts as a judge, so we don't need people labeling every response.   Two cautions: The judge can be wrong. Spot-check a sample of its scores by hand, especially early on. Recall still needs reference answers. Budget time to write a small ground-truth set for your test questions.  AWS doesn't offer a managed Ragas service. Ragas is an open-source library, and AWS supports it in three ways: Bedrock models can act as its judge, AWS compute and tracking services can run it, and Bedrock has a native evaluation feature that covers much of the same ground. 1. Amazon Bedrock as the Ragas judge and embedding model This is the most direct integration. Ragas' LLM-based metrics (faithfulness, context recall, and so on) need an evaluator LLM, and the Ragas docs show wrapping a Bedrock model (via LangChain's ChatBedrockConverse) as the evaluator LLM, with a Titan model for embeddings. Your eval code stays standard Ragas; only the judge changes.  2. Bedrock Knowledge Bases evaluation (native alternative) Bedrock has a built-in evaluation job type for Knowledge Bases, generally available since March 2025, that uses a foundation model as judge and computes the metrics without any Ragas or custom scoring code. ## Slide 34
> Notes: Presentation Notes — Use Case: Enterprise Technical Support System Opening / setup This graphic illustrates how the AI development spectrum can become a real production architecture. Imagine a company that manufactures or operates industrial equipment—motors, pumps, compressors, turbines, or manufacturing machinery. When equipment begins behaving abnormally, an engineer needs to determine three things quickly: What is happening? Why is it happening? And what should we do about it? The key point in this example is that we don’t necessarily choose between prompt engineering, RAG, fine-tuning, and traditional machine learning. A mature AI system may combine all four because each solves a different part of the problem. Stage 1 — Prompt Engineering: Start Simple We begin on the left with prompt engineering. Suppose an engineer reports: “The motor is overheating and shutting down. What could be the cause, and how do I fix it?” We can immediately give that question to a capable foundation model using a carefully constructed troubleshooting prompt. The prompt might instruct the model to behave as an industrial maintenance assistant, ask clarifying questions, identify likely causes, and provide a systematic diagnostic procedure. This is attractive because we can have a prototype running very quickly—potentially within hours. Notice that we haven’t trained anything. We’re leveraging capabilities already present in the foundation model. But we quickly encounter a limitation: the model knows general engineering concepts, but it doesn’t necessarily know our equipment, our maintenance history, or our company’s procedures. That takes us to Stage 2. Stage 2 — RAG: Add Company Knowledge Now we introduce Retrieval-Augmented Generation, or RAG.  The company may have thousands of equipment manuals, service bulletins, maintenance records, troubleshooting guides, and other internal documents. Rather than attempting to put all of this information into a prompt—or retraining the model every time a document changes—we create a searchable knowledge base. When the engineer submits a question, the system retrieves the most relevant information at runtime and supplies it to the foundation model as context. Now the model isn’t answering only from what it learned during its original training. It can answer using the organization’s current documentation. This gives us an important distinction: Prompt engineering tells the model how we want it to approach the problem. RAG gives the model the information it needs to solve the problem. And critically, RAG still doesn’t require us to modify the model’s weights. Stage 3 — Fine-Tuning: Change Model Behavior Eventually we may discover another problem. Perhaps the model has access to the correct information but doesn’t consistently follow the company’s diagnostic methodology. Maybe engineers must perform tests in a specific order, classify failures using an internal taxonomy, or produce reports according to a strict schema. This is where fine-tuning may become appropriate. We can collect historical examples of expert diagnoses, troubleshooting cases, step-by-step procedures, correct diagnoses, and examples of properly structured outputs. We then use those examples to adapt the foundation model. The distinction from RAG is worth emphasizing:  RAG primarily supplies knowledge. Fine-tuning primarily changes model behavior. After fine-tuning, we want the model to respond more consistently according to the organization’s specialized diagnostic process. But one important source of information is still missing from this architecture. The equipment itself. Stage 4 — Custom Predictive Models: Use the Sensor Data Modern industrial equipment may generate enormous amounts of sensor data: temperature, vibration, pressure, voltage, operating history, and previous failure information. This data is fundamentally different from manuals and troubleshooting documents. Suppose vibration has been slowly increasing for six weeks while motor temperature has simultaneously risen under a particular load condition. An LLM isn’t necessarily the best tool for discovering that predictive pattern. Instead, we might train a specialized machine-learning model on historical sensor data and equipment failures. That could be a traditional tabular model, a time-series model, or another specialized predictive architecture. Its job might be to estimate equipment health or identify the probability of an impending failure. This illustrates an important design principle:  Not every AI problem is an LLM problem. We choose the model architecture that fits the data and the prediction task. Putting Everything Together The bottom half of the graphic is the most important part. Notice that the production architecture contains multiple models working together. An engineer’s question enters the AI application. Prompt engineering interprets and orchestrates the request. RAG retrieves relevant company knowledge. A fine-tuned model produces a response following the organization’s diagnostic procedures. Meanwhile, a predictive model analyzes the equipment’s sensor data. The system can then combine those results. Instead of simply producing a generic chatbot response, the engineer might receive: a diagnosis and possible root cause, a step-by-step troubleshooting procedure, the relevant equipment manuals and service documents, an assessment of equipment health and potential failure risk, and recommended actions and next steps. That is a much more interesting system than simply putting a chat interface in front of an LLM.  Architecture insight — These Stages Are Cumulative There’s another subtle point in the graphic. When we move from Stage 1 to Stage 2, we don’t necessarily throw away prompt engineering. When we introduce fine-tuning, we don’t necessarily throw away RAG. And when we introduce a predictive model, we don’t necessarily replace the generative model. Instead, the architecture becomes compositional. We might ultimately have: Prompting + RAG + Fine-Tuning + Predictive ML with each component doing the job for which it is best suited. This is why the development spectrum shouldn’t be interpreted simply as a maturity ladder where every sophisticated application eventually reaches the right-hand side. The correct architecture depends on the problem. Discussion question for students At this point, I would ask: If RAG gives the model access to the company’s troubleshooting manuals, why would we still need fine-tuning? The distinction I want students to recognize is that having access to the right information doesn’t guarantee that the model will exhibit the right behavior. RAG can retrieve the company’s required diagnostic procedure. Fine-tuning can make following that procedure a more consistent characteristic of the model’s behavior. Then ask a second question:  Why not simply give the sensor data to the LLM and let it predict equipment failures? This opens the discussion about model selection. If we have millions of historical sensor observations and known failure outcomes, a specialized predictive model may offer advantages in accuracy, efficiency, validation, and monitoring for that particular task. Closing takeaway The lesson from this architecture is: Don’t ask, “Which AI model should we use?” Ask, “What capabilities does the system require, and which model or technique is best suited to each capability?” Real enterprise AI systems increasingly look less like one giant intelligent model and more like an orchestrated collection of specialized components. Prompt engineering provides instruction and orchestration.RAG provides current and proprietary knowledge.Fine-tuning provides specialized behavior and consistency.Predictive models provide specialized analysis of structured and time-series data. The engineering challenge is learning how to make those pieces work together as one reliable system.

> Notes: KEY POINTS (say these; the detail below is reference) •  MLOps operates a model artifact; LLMOps operates a system built around a model someone else owns. •  Differences: primary artifact, cost profile, evaluation, version control, output determinism. •  Shared principles remain: versioning, gates, monitoring, rollback.  Presentation Notes — MLOps vs. LLMOps Opening:This slide highlights why LLMOps is related to MLOps, but it isn't simply MLOps with a larger model. Both disciplines are concerned with getting AI systems into production reliably. They share ideas such as versioning, evaluation, monitoring, deployment controls, and observability. But the thing we're operating—and therefore what we need to control—is substantially different. The table compares those differences across five dimensions.  1. Primary artifact — What are we actually managing? Start with the first row. In traditional MLOps, the central artifact is typically the trained model—its weights, architecture, hyperparameters, and associated preprocessing logic. For our NorthStar churn model, for example, we train an XGBoost model. We produce model.tar.gz, evaluate it, register it, and ultimately deploy that specific model artifact. With LLMOps, that's often not what we're doing. If we're using a hosted foundation model, we usually don't own or train the base-model weights. Instead, much of our application behavior comes from the things surrounding the model: prompts, system instructions, retrieval configuration, tools, model parameters, guardrails, and orchestration logic. For a RAG application, I would also include the retrieval index and knowledge corpus as important versioned artifacts. So the shift is roughly: MLOps: operate the model.LLMOps: operate the system around the model. That's a major architectural difference. 2. Cost profile — Where does the money go? The second row shows another important difference. Traditional ML frequently has a significant training cost, especially as model size and dataset size increase. Once a relatively lightweight model is trained, however, individual predictions may be inexpensive. For something like NorthStar's XGBoost churn model, that pattern is particularly clear: training is a discrete job, while inference is lightweight. Hosted foundation models often reverse some of that operational emphasis. The provider has absorbed the enormous cost of pretraining the foundation model. We consume it through inference. That means our costs increasingly depend on things such as: tokens, requests, context-window size, model choice, retrieval volume, and agent/tool usage. At small scale, this may be inexpensive. At enterprise volume, inference costs can become substantial. That's why FinOps becomes tightly connected to LLMOps. Token usage and model routing aren't merely engineering details—they become production cost controls. One nuance: this row describes a common hosted-LLM pattern, not a universal rule. Fine-tuning or self-hosting foundation models can shift the cost profile significantly. 3. Evaluation — What does “correct” mean? This is probably the most important difference on the slide. For conventional predictive ML, evaluation is often based on relatively objective metrics. For classification we might use: accuracy, precision, recall, F1, or ROC-AUC. For regression we might use RMSE or MAE. And we usually have labeled ground truth against which we can compare the prediction. Our NorthStar churn model is a good example. We can ask: “Did this customer actually churn?” and compare that outcome to the prediction. With generative AI, there often isn't one correct answer. Two responses can be completely different and both be good. So LLM evaluation becomes multidimensional. We may need to evaluate: correctness, relevance, coherence, groundedness, helpfulness, safety, style, and adherence to instructions. For RAG, we may separately evaluate retrieval quality and answer faithfulness. And because many of these qualities are difficult to capture with one deterministic metric, evaluation may combine automated tests, LLM-as-judge, and human evaluation. That is why LLMOps requires a much richer evaluation framework. 4. Version control and ownership The fourth row changes the boundary of what we control. With conventional MLOps, if we've trained the model ourselves, we have substantial control over the model artifact. We can version: training code, data, features, hyperparameters, model weights, checkpoints, and environment. That gives us a strong reproducibility story. With a hosted foundation model, the provider typically controls the underlying model. Our responsibility becomes versioning everything we control around it. That includes: prompts, configurations, retrieval indexes, embedding choices, tools, guardrails, evaluation sets, and application code. We should also pin a specific provider model/version whenever the service allows it. This matters because a foundation-model application is not reproducible merely because we put the prompt in Git. The whole compound AI system has to be versioned. 5. Output characteristics — Predictability changes The last row gets at one of the reasons LLMOps evaluation is harder. Traditional predictive models are often operationally deterministic at inference: given the same model, preprocessing, and input, we generally expect the same prediction. Generative models are commonly used in a way that allows variation. The same prompt can produce different wording—or sometimes meaningfully different answers—depending on sampling configuration and model behavior. Parameters such as temperature can influence that variation. That creates an important operational consequence: A test cannot always say, “The output must equal this exact string.” Instead, LLMOps testing often needs to ask: “Does this response satisfy the required properties?” Is it grounded? Is it safe? Does it answer the question? Does it follow the required format? Does it cite the correct source? That's a very different testing philosophy. One technical qualification is worth making here: non-determinism isn't absolute. LLM outputs can often be made much more stable with constrained decoding and fixed configurations, while some traditional ML systems can also exhibit nondeterminism. The slide is describing the typical operational distinction. What MLOps and LLMOps still have in common I don't want this comparison to imply that these are unrelated disciplines. Both still need: version control, automated testing, evaluation gates, CI/CD, monitoring, rollback strategies, security, governance, observability, and cost management. The difference is what we version, what we evaluate, what can fail, and what we monitor. That's why it makes sense to think of LLMOps as an extension of the broader XOps philosophy rather than a completely independent discipline. Connection to NorthStar This distinction becomes concrete when we compare two NorthStar systems. For the churn model, we're operating a trained XGBoost artifact. The lifecycle looks like: Feature Store → Train → Evaluate AUC → Gate → Registry → Deploy → Monitor. For the offer-generation RAG system, the operational object is much broader. Now we're managing: prompts + foundation model selection + embeddings + vector index + retrieved context + generation parameters + guardrails + evaluations. And that changes both our CI/CD pipeline and our production monitoring. Key takeaway The sentence I'd leave students with is: MLOps primarily operationalizes a model artifact; LLMOps operationalizes a compound AI system built around a foundation model. That difference explains why LLMOps places much greater emphasis on prompt and configuration versioning, retrieval, multidimensional evaluation, safety, observability, and inference cost. The operational principles remain familiar. But once we move from predictive models to generative systems, the artifact gets broader, evaluation gets less binary, and the production surface we have to manage gets much larger.

## Slide 41
- XOps: AWS Services Stack
> Notes: KEY POINTS (say these; the detail below is reference) •  Each discipline operates a different artifact: data, model, foundation-model application, agent actions. •  The DataOps column is a reference stack. NorthStar uses Glue Workflows and CloudWatch in place of MWAA, dbt and Glue Data Quality. •  CI/CD, identity, observability, governance and cost controls are shared across all four.  Presentation Notes Opening This slide gives us the big-picture view of the XOps stack. The important idea is that DataOps, MLOps, LLMOps, and AgentOps are not four competing architectures. They address different operational problems at different layers of an AI system. As we move from left to right, the artifact we're operating becomes progressively more complex. DataOps — trusted data Start with DataOps. The primary artifact here is data. Our objective is to make that data trustworthy, reproducible, discoverable, and usable downstream. That requires orchestration, transformation, quality checks, cataloging and lineage, and event-driven execution. In our AWS example, that might involve MWAA, Glue and dbt, Glue Data Quality, SageMaker Catalog, and EventBridge. The output isn't merely data. It is: Validated, discoverable data that downstream systems can trust. Everything else depends on that foundation. MLOps — reliable models MLOps builds on that data foundation, but now our operational artifact is the machine-learning model and its lifecycle. We need repeatable training pipelines, experiment tracking, consistent features, evaluation gates, a Model Registry, deployment, and production monitoring. For NorthStar, this is where the pieces from Labs 2, 3, and 4 start coming together. Our Feature Store gives us consistent features. SageMaker Pipelines automates training and evaluation. MLflow or SageMaker Experiments provides experiment tracking. The Model Registry governs candidate models. Model Monitor and our serving infrastructure help us operate those models after deployment. So the MLOps outcome is: Governed model artifacts and reliable production endpoints. LLMOps — foundation-model applications Now we move into LLMOps. The artifact changes again. In many foundation-model applications, we don't own or retrain the underlying model weights. Instead, we operate the system surrounding the foundation model. That means versioning prompts and configurations, continuously evaluating responses, enforcing guardrails, monitoring application behavior, tracking token usage and cost, and managing model access. For an AWS implementation, Amazon Bedrock becomes an important serving layer, with Bedrock evaluation and Guardrails supporting the operational lifecycle. The output of LLMOps is therefore not simply an LLM. It is a: Governed foundation-model application—with controlled prompts, evaluation, access, safety, observability, and cost. AgentOps — safe autonomous execution AgentOps introduces another level of complexity. An agent doesn't merely generate text. It can reason, call tools, access memory, invoke APIs, and take actions. That means our operational concern shifts from monitoring outputs to monitoring execution. We need to know: What did the agent do? Which tools did it call? What happened inside the workflow? How long did each step take? Did it exceed its authority? When should a human approve an action? And what happens when something fails? This is why tracing becomes so important in AgentOps. In the current AWS architecture, AgentCore provides capabilities specifically intended to operate and observe agents, including telemetry that integrates with CloudWatch and OpenTelemetry. The desired outcome is: Governed agent actions and observable execution. Notice the progression Look across the four columns. The progression is roughly: DataOps: Can I trust the data? MLOps: Can I trust the model? LLMOps: Can I trust the foundation-model application? AgentOps: Can I trust the actions the autonomous system takes? That's a useful mental model for distinguishing these disciplines. But don't interpret this as four silos The arrows say “builds on” rather than implying a literal data pipeline. That's deliberate. Not every LLM system needs a custom ML model, and not every agent requires every capability shown in the preceding columns. Instead, the point is that the operational disciplines accumulate. AgentOps doesn't eliminate LLMOps. LLMOps doesn't eliminate MLOps. And none of them eliminate the need for reliable data. The shared XOps foundation Finally, notice the bar across the bottom. All four disciplines share some common operational foundations: CI/CD, identity and access, observability, governance, and cost controls. This is one reason I prefer thinking about these disciplines collectively as XOps. We're repeatedly applying many of the same operational principles—automation, testing, observability, governance, reproducibility, and ownership—to different AI artifacts. Key takeaway The takeaway is: XOps is not one tool or one pipeline. It is a family of operational disciplines applied to increasingly complex AI artifacts. A simple way to remember the stack is: DataOps → trust the dataMLOps → trust the model lifecycleLLMOps → trust the foundation-model applicationAgentOps → trust the autonomous execution And as we move to the right, observability and governance become more important—not less—because the system gains greater ability to make decisions and take actions.
