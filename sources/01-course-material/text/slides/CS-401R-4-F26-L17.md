> Notes: Question: Of the attackers on the last slide, what does each half of this slide do about them?  Let the room map it before you explain and point them to the verbs on the red tags: identity limits, while the network keeps out. The difference matters. Least privilege does not stop an insider or a bad dependency from acting. It limits what they can reach once they do.  In Lab 2, students built three least-privilege roles. MLEngineer covers SageMaker training and serving and the features/ and artifacts/ prefixes, but not Athena or Glue.  DataEngineer covers Glue and the data prefixes.  ModelMonitor gets CloudWatch plus read-only access to artifacts.  Walk through what each role contains. An insider holding DataEngineer can corrupt raw or processed data but cannot train, register or deploy a model.  Someone with ModelMonitor credentials can read but cannot change anything.  And because MLEngineer cannot write to raw/ or processed/, an attack stays in its own area.  That containment has a limit, and the gap box shows where it fails. A malicious package in the training container runs as MLEngineer, so it can overwrite artifacts/ and swap the model. Since MLEngineer also serves, it can then deploy that model. An insider holding MLEngineer can do the same thing, with no second person involved at any point.  Ask: which of your roles could push a model to production today? After they answer, explain that the enterprise design closes this gap in two ways: a separate approver role in the Model Registry, and deployment only through the pipeline. Even then, a compromised dependency is only contained. This is exactly the kind of gap Lab 5 Task 3a asks for. At least one STRIDE threat must name the role that contains the blast radius, and another must name a gap the roles do not close.  On the network side, the verb is keeps out, with one caveat. An external attacker with stolen credentials becomes an authorized caller, which is why identity and network must work together.  Start with the default, because it’s the problem. SageMaker resources can reach the internet by default, and that is unacceptable for an endpoint handling customer data. A training job could exfiltrate data, a container could pull malicious code, and Bedrock calls would leave the AWS boundary.   The diagram shows the enterprise target: private subnets, with VPC endpoints as the only egress. Your own platform stops one step short of that. Lab 1 put Studio in a public subnet, and Lab 2 moved it into private subnets that reach the internet through a NAT gateway you control. Ask: what does a VPC endpoint give you that a NAT gateway does not? A NAT gateway gives your private subnets a way out to anywhere. A VPC endpoint gives them a private path to one specific AWS service, and lets you control who can use that path and what they can reach through it.  The code shows how this applies to what you actually deploy. The network settings go on the model, not on a training job. vpc_config on the approved ModelPackage becomes the endpoint’s VpcConfig, so the endpoint’s containers get no internet route. That fixes Activity 1’s “no VpcConfig” defect. Training jobs take the same subnets and security group, so in production both training and serving run inside private subnets.  That leads to one practical gotcha. Suppose you put a Lambda in a private subnet and call Bedrock without the com.amazonaws.us-east-1.bedrock-runtime interface endpoint. The call won’t throw a clear error. It just fails silently when it times out.  Transition: Identity and network are the two things that control who can reach the system. Together they decide who gets in. The next slide moves from preventing access to proving what happened to the data after someone did get in, which presumably means audit logs and lineage.

> Notes: Question: If compliance asks what happened to a customer's data, how do you prove it?  These two controls are one idea. Encryption controls who could see the data; audit logging proves who did. A compliance reviewer asks both in the same breath.  At rest, everything uses the customer-managed key northstar-ai-kms-key. In transit, S3 bucket policy denies plain HTTP and the SageMaker and Bedrock APIs require TLS 1.2 or higher. VPC-endpoint traffic never leaves the AWS network.  Note that your own Lab 1 bucket uses SSE-S3, not a customer-managed KMS key. SSE-S3 stands for Server-Side Encryption with Amazon S3-managed keys. “Server-side” means S3 encrypts your data after receiving it and before writing it to disk, then decrypts it when it’s read. That’s the opposite of client-side encryption, where you encrypt the data before uploading it. The bucket is encrypted, but AWS owns and manages the key. You haven’t created and managed a key of your own in AWS KMS. In both cases the data on disk is encrypted with AES-256. What changes is who controls the key, and so who controls access through it. That is exactly the choice Lab 5 Task 3b asks you to justify asset by asset.  The real control is the PII architecture. Direct identifiers are stripped at ETL; the customer becomes a synthetic customer_id. The Feature Store holds PII-free features only. Inference looks up features by customer_id, so PII never enters the inference path. Even a full compromise of model artifacts and the feature store yields synthetic IDs and aggregated behavior, not customers. That indirection is the highest-leverage privacy decision in the whole architecture, and it costs one join.  Audit has four sources: CloudTrail answers who deployed.  Model Registry metadata (git commit, training job, dataset version, evaluation report, trained_by) answers from which code and data.  The approval workflow answers who approved.  Data capture on 20 percent of requests answers why it predicted that.  Seven-year retention is not arbitrary: retail financial transaction records are commonly subject to 5-to-7-year retention rules, and prediction logs that drive discount offers link to those transactions. Glacier Deep Archive is about $0.00099 per GB-month, so cost is never the reason to keep less. One accuracy note: Athena cannot query objects in Deep Archive directly; recent logs stay queryable in S3 and age into Deep Archive via a lifecycle rule, with a restore needed for older audits. ## Slide 7
- SSE-S3 vs. Customer-Managed KMS Key
- Same: AES-256 at rest, applied server-side by S3, invisible to your code. Different: who controls the key.
> Notes: What do you actually gain by managing your own encryption key instead of letting S3 do it?  Both options encrypt data at rest with AES-256, and both are server-side: S3 encrypts when it writes and decrypts when it reads, so your code never changes. The difference is who controls the key.  With SSE-S3, AWS owns the key. That protects against stolen disks, but anyone with s3:GetObject can read the data, so encryption adds no second gate.  With a customer-managed KMS key, reading an object needs two permissions: s3:GetObject and kms:Decrypt. A bucket-policy mistake alone no longer exposes data. You also get a CloudTrail record of every decrypt, and a kill switch: disable the key and the data is unreadable.  The cost is money and friction: about a dollar per key per month plus request fees, and the most common "Access Denied" in SageMaker pipelines is a missing kms:Decrypt grant.  Our lab platform uses SSE-S3 to keep things simple; an enterprise holding PII would use a customer-managed key.

> Notes: Question: How much redundancy does a model endpoint need, and how do you decide?  Pick by SLA, not by instinct. Each tier up the ladder survives a bigger failure, and each one costs more.  Start at the bottom. Tier 1 is more than one instance. You do not add a load balancer: a SageMaker endpoint balances across its own instances, as L16 showed. Tier 2 is Availability Zones, and on SageMaker the two tiers come together: give a variant two or more instances and SageMaker spreads them across AZs for you. A single instance has neither. In Lab 5 your quota of two ml.m5.large instances is just enough for that.  Tier 3 is a second region, active-active or active-passive. The table on the right shows why the dashed line sits where it does. 99.9% allows about 44 minutes of downtime a month, which instance and AZ redundancy can meet. 99.99% allows about four minutes, and a single region cannot promise that, because a regional outage alone would use it up. Above 99.9%, regional redundancy is required.  Tier 4 is the one unique to AI, and the one nobody builds. Push on it: you have a backup database; do you have a backup model? When the XGBoost endpoint is down at 2 a.m., is there a logistic regression that can serve at 80% of the quality, or does the business process just stop? The reason it matters is psychological as much as technical: without a fallback, every model incident becomes an emergency, and emergency retraining is how bad models reach production. The backup model buys time to fix the real one properly.  Transition: Let's test both ideas on a real failure in the NorthStar serving path.

- Circuit Breakers: Fail Fast, Serve the Fallback
- Three states, four transitions
- Half-open
- Closed
- Open
- failures ≥ threshold
- probe succeeds
- timeout elapses
- probe fails
- Closed
- Calls pass through; failures are counted
- Open
- Calls skip the dependency; the fallback answers at once

- A few probe calls test whether it has recovered
- Tune three numbers: call timeout · failure threshold · how long to stay Open
- 12
> Notes: Question: What stops one slow dependency from taking down the whole AI system?  A circuit breaker sits in front of a dependency and watches its failures. It works like the breaker in your house: when something downstream goes wrong, it trips, so the damage does not spread.  Walk the three states. Closed is normal: calls pass through and failures are counted. When failures cross the threshold, it trips to Open: calls skip the dependency entirely and the fallback answers at once, so nothing waits on a service that is already struggling. After a set time it moves to Half-open and lets a few probe calls through. If they succeed it closes; if they fail, the dashed red arrow, it goes straight back to Open.  Now the table. Every external dependency in an AI system needs one, and each fails differently without it. An LLM API that slows down makes requests pile up and exhaust the thread pool. An agent calling a dead tool keeps retrying until its token budget runs out, which ties back to L09 and L11: a per-tool breaker is the difference between an agent that degrades gracefully and one that burns its budget on a dead endpoint. A slow feature store slows every prediction with it.  The fallback column is the design decision. A breaker with nothing to fall back to just turns a slow failure into a fast error. The fallback is what keeps the business running.  The bottom bar names the three numbers you tune: the call timeout, the failure threshold, and how long to stay Open. Do not over-explain the timeout here; Activity 2 tests it in a minute.  Transition: Breakers contain a failing dependency. Redundancy is the other half: surviving when your own serving capacity fails.

> Notes: Question: How much data can you afford to lose, and how long can you afford to be down?  Both numbers are business decisions, not technical ones.  RPO, recovery point objective, is how much data you can afford to lose, measured as time back to the last good copy. For most production AI systems the target is close to zero, and two habits get you there. First, save the inputs before you process them: customer data in versioned S3 and the Feature Store, the scoring file in S3, and real-time requests and predictions through data capture. Second, record progress as you go: a checkpoint marks which records or pipeline steps are done. When something fails, you restart from the last checkpoint and replay everything after it from the saved inputs. The model is deterministic, so you get the same scores. Nothing is lost; it just finishes later.  Point at the thin teal mark at the failure line on each track: that is the data lost, almost none. It is the same sliver for both systems. The difference between them is entirely on the right, in recovery time.  Why approximately zero and not exactly: a real-time request in flight at the moment of failure can be lost unless the caller retries, and anything you never logged, such as predictions with data capture off from Activity 1, cannot be replayed.  RTO, recovery time objective, is acceptable downtime, set by the system's role in the business process. Payment fraud detection: minutes, because every minute down is fraud written straight to the balance sheet. Nightly churn scoring: hours, provided the scored file lands before the marketing platform's 06:00 window. That is the teaching example: the deadline is when the downstream process reads the file. Ask: "What is the RTO for your team project's system? Not the number that sounds responsible; the number the business process actually forces."  The runbook recovers the full system from nothing: restore artifacts from S3, rebuild the endpoint from Terraform, restore feature pipeline state, run smoke tests, confirm monitoring, notify stakeholders. A runbook that has never been executed is documentation, not capability. In Lab 6 you will write one, and the question I will ask is whether you ran it. This also pairs with Lab 5's rollback requirement: students who can state RPO and RTO write much better rollback triggers. ## Slide 19
- Production Readiness Review (PRR)
> Notes: A Production Readiness Review, or PRR, is the formal checkpoint before an AI system reaches production. It asks whether we can launch and operate the entire system safely.  Start on the left with evidence. The team brings evaluation results, data quality checks, security controls, and responsible AI assessments. It also demonstrates that the infrastructure can handle expected demand, fallback procedures work, and someone owns rollback, runbooks, and on-call support.  In the center, reviewers assess that evidence against criteria agreed in advance. This is a cross-functional decision involving technical, business, and operational owners. Review depth should match the system’s risk: a customer-facing agent authorized to issue refunds needs more scrutiny than an internal recommendation tool.  On the right are three possible outcomes: Go: The system is ready for the planned rollout. Go with conditions: A limited release is permitted with safeguards, named owners, and deadlines. Critical blockers must already be resolved. No-go: Release is blocked until the team fixes the gaps and resubmits evidence, as the red return arrow shows.  For example, a churn model could pass its accuracy threshold but still receive a no-go because rollback has never been tested or no operational owner has accepted responsibility. Every outcome becomes a recorded decision: what was reviewed, the supporting evidence, who approved it, and any conditions. Material changes to the model, data, infrastructure, or use case trigger reassessment. The key message is that production readiness combines model quality, operational capability, and clear accountability.

Personal data inventory: what personal data is processed, and at which stage of the pipeline Students trace customer data through the platform they built: Ingestion (raw/): names, emails and transaction history arrive with direct identifiers.
