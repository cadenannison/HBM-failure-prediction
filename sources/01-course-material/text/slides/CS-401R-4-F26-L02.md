## Slide 7
- Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC)
- Planning – Define project goals, scope, resources, schedules, risks, and feasibility.
- Requirements Analysis – Identify and document functional and non-functional requirements based on stakeholder needs.
- Design – Define the software architecture, components, interfaces, data models, and technical approach.
- Development – Implement the design by writing, integrating, and reviewing software code.
- Testing and Quality Assurance – Verify that the software meets requirements and identify defects, security issues, and performance problems.
- Deployment – Release the software into production using controlled and repeatable processes.
- Operations and Maintenance – Monitor, support, troubleshoot, patch, and enhance the software throughout its operational life.

- 1

## Slide 15
- Example: What is the right problemAT&T Customer Churn
> Notes: For example, while working for AT&T on customer churn we thought that if we just had a model to predict who would churn, then we could use that model to make significant impacts in churn reduction. But what was the real problem?   If we could predict someone would churn, we still might not improve retention; why?   Some of the subscribers were going to churn anyway, no matter what we did. Others were going to stay, no matter what we did. Instead of predicting churners, we actually needed to segment out those who were either out the door or loyalists. Also, there was a segment we called “let sleeping dogs lie.” That is, any contact could cause them to wake up and churn. So we focused on modeling which customers that were predicted to churn would change their minds based on the marketing treatment (e.g., free phone, additional data, etc.). We needed to know of those likely to churn, who would positively respond to a given marketing treatment. That was the real problem. Also, we had the constraint that the marketing treatment needed to be financially justified.

> Notes: Key activities: Inventory all available data sources: structured (transaction databases, CRM systems), unstructured (call center transcripts, support tickets), internal, and external. Assess data quality across five dimensions: accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, and accessibility. Can you actually access it in a compliant way? Assess label quality. For supervised learning, how is the target variable defined? Is there a label delay, where you will not know whether a customer churned until the observation window closes? Is there label leakage risk from features that encode future information? Is the ground truth reliable? Identify data gaps and assess the cost and timeline to close them. Validate data access rights and governance. Is this data legally and contractually available for the intended use? Are there PII or regulatory constraints? Produce a Data Card (or equivalent) for each primary dataset, covering provenance, consent status, known quality issues, restrictions on use, and known biases in the collection methodology. Prototype data pipelines to validate assumptions before committing to the build. Do not take data availability on faith.  Gate question: Is the data sufficient to support the problem as defined? If not, what would it take to make it sufficient, and is that investment justified? Has label quality been assessed and accepted?  Why this gate is critical: Insufficient or mislabeled data is among the most common and most avoidable causes of AI project failure  Teams that skip data validation build elaborate model training pipelines only to discover that the data cannot support the required performance. A project with insufficient data should not proceed, however enthusiastic the business is.  Risk flag: Data that exists in theory but is not accessible in practice is not available data. That includes data siloed in legacy systems, owned by another business unit, or governed by contracts that prohibit ML use. Prototype the pipeline before passing the gate.  Key output: Data Readiness Assessment, covering available data sources, quality scores by dimension, label quality assessment, identified gaps, access risks, and a clear go/no-go recommendation. These scores can and should be automated and aligned with business requirements and standards.

> Notes: Scope: Move the validated system into production in a controlled, observable, and recoverable way.  Key activities: Deploy using established CI/CD practices, promoting the model artifact through staging before production (Ch @sec:build_cicd). Select and implement a deployment strategy: canary release (route 5 to 10% of traffic to the new model first), blue-green deployment (keep the old model as a live fallback), shadow mode (run the new model in parallel without affecting users), or A/B testing (split traffic to measure business impact). Activate monitoring, alerting, and observability from day one rather than as a follow-on task (Part 4). Define explicit rollback triggers before launch, meaning conditions that automatically or immediately prompt a rollback to the prior system. Examples: canary performance drops below baseline; data pipeline SLA failure; off-brand or policy-violating content rate exceeds the threshold; protected segment disparity exceeds the accepted level; inference cost exceeds the ceiling for consecutive days. Write rollback criteria before deployment, not during an incident. (Ch 4.4). Complete the Operational Handoff Package for the team that will operate the system after deployment, and confirm that the team has been trained and has accepted the handoff. The handoff is itself a gate. Deployment does not pass until operations has formally accepted ownership.  The Operational Handoff Package includes the Model Card, Data Cards for primary training datasets, system architecture documentation, monitoring dashboard links and alert threshold documentation, the runbook library, escalation contacts, inference cost baseline and ceiling, and on-call training materials.  Gate question: Is the system behaving as expected in production? Are all monitoring thresholds active and calibrated? Has the operations team accepted the handoff?  Why deployment strategy matters: Silent failure, where the system keeps serving predictions while performance degrades below any meaningful threshold, is a real and underappreciated risk in AI systems. A canary deployment with monitoring catches that class of failure before it reaches the full user population.  Key outputs: Deployed system, monitoring dashboards, runbooks, Operational Handoff Package (including Model Card and rollback criteria)

## Slide 43
- AI Strategy Portfolio MAP Template
- 43
> Notes: An AI Strategy Portfolio Map is a management framework for organizing, comparing, and prioritizing an organization’s AI initiatives as a portfolio of strategic investments rather than as a collection of disconnected AI projects.  One of the biggest mistakes in AI strategy is asking: “Which AI use case has the highest ROI?” That question tends to favor relatively safe automation projects. Those projects can be valuable, but an organization that selects only high-confidence ROI projects can end up with an AI strategy consisting entirely of incremental efficiency improvements.  The portfolio question is better: “What combination of AI investments gives us the right balance of near-term value, organizational learning, scalable competitive advantage, and long-term strategic options?”

## Slide 55
- There are many different AI Lifecycle Processes
- 55
- De Silva, Alahakoon, Center for Data Analytics and Cognition (CDAC) 2023
- Toborg 2023
- MS Team Data Science Process (TDSP)
- CDAC
> Notes: Data-Driven Development vs. Conventional Programming. CRISP-DM, SEMMA, and KDD are three different methodologies or frameworks that are commonly used in the field of data science and machine learning to guide the process of developing and deploying data-driven solutions. Let's take a closer look at each of them: CRISP-DM (Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining): CRISP-DM is a widely used and well-established methodology for data mining and analytics projects. It consists of six phases: Business Understanding: Understanding the project objectives and requirements from a business perspective. Data Understanding: Collecting and exploring the available data to gain insights into its quality and structure. Data Preparation: Preprocessing and cleaning the data, transforming it into a suitable format for analysis. Modeling: Creating and training predictive models using various algorithms and techniques. Evaluation: Assessing the performance and effectiveness of the models using validation and testing. Deployment: Integrating the chosen model into the business environment and making it operational. (What are the problems with CRISP-DM? The top four problems are a lack of clarity, mindless rework, blind hand-offs to IT and a failure to iterate. Decision modeling and decision management can address these problems, maximizing the value of CRISP-DM and ensuring analytic success.)  SEMMA (Sample, Explore, Modify, Model, Assess): SEMMA is a methodology developed by the SAS Institute for data mining and analytics. It consists of five stages: Sample: Selecting a representative sample of the data for analysis. Explore: Exploring the data to identify patterns, trends, and potential relationships. Modify: Preprocessing and cleaning the data, handling missing values, and transforming variables. Model: Building and training predictive models to make predictions or classifications. Assess: Evaluating the model's performance and effectiveness on unseen data.  KDD (Knowledge Discovery in Databases): KDD is a broader concept that encompasses the entire process of discovering knowledge from large datasets. It is not just a methodology but rather a comprehensive framework for data mining. The KDD process includes several steps: Selection: Choosing relevant data from the available sources. Preprocessing: Cleaning, transforming, and reducing the data to a manageable size. Transformation: Converting the data into suitable formats for analysis. Data Mining: Applying algorithms and techniques to discover patterns, relationships, and insights. Interpretation/Evaluation: Interpreting the results, evaluating their significance, and assessing their usefulness. Knowledge Presentation: Visualizing and communicating the discovered knowledge to stakeholders. (KDDS can be a useful expansion for big data teams. However, KDDS only addresses some of the shortcomings of CRISP-DM. For example, it is not clear how a team should iterate when using KDDS. In addition, its combination of phases and processes is less straight-forward. Adoption of KDDS outside of SAIC is not known.)  In summary, CRISP-DM, SEMMA, and KDD are all methodologies that provide structured approaches to guide the process of extracting insights and knowledge from data. CRISP-DM is widely used and focuses on the end-to-end process of data mining. SEMMA, developed by SAS, emphasizes data preparation and modeling. KDD is a broader framework that covers the entire knowledge discovery process, including data selection, preprocessing, mining, interpretation, and presentation. The choice of methodology often depends on the specific project's requirements and the tools or software used in the organization.  There are many different development models. The one we are teaching is a comprehensive model that covers both single applications and production of many models. It also explicitly addresses the need for continuous iteration from Concept through full Production. In reality most applications may not use all of the phases and stages as described but will be simplified to meet business circumstances.
