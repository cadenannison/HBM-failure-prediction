## Slide 8
- Goodhart’s Law: When A Metric Replaces the Goal
> Notes: Start by emphasizing the difference between the true goal and the metric we can easily measure. An AI recommendation system should help users find content they consider useful and satisfying. Click-through rate, or CTR, measures the percentage of displayed recommendations that users click. It is easy to track, but clicking does not tell us whether the content was worth the user’s time.  Initially, the metric works. As recommendations become more relevant, users click more often and find more value. CTR and satisfaction improve together, so optimizing CTR seems reasonable.  The weak form appears when that relationship stops being informative. Better headlines or more prominent placement may attract additional clicks without making the recommended content more useful. CTR continues to rise while satisfaction stays flat. The system has improved its score, but we no longer have evidence that it has improved the user experience.  The response is to recalibrate the measurement approach. Collect feedback about whether recommendations were useful or worth the user’s time, and check whether CTR still predicts that value. Simply replacing CTR with another proxy is not a permanent solution; the relationship between the metric and the goal needs ongoing validation.  The strong form appears when optimizing the metric makes the real outcome worse. A recommender rewarded primarily for clicks may learn to favor misleading or sensational content. People click because the headline promises something compelling, then feel disappointed by what they receive. CTR rises while satisfaction and trust decline.  The model does not need to intend deception. If clickbait earns a stronger reward than useful content, the optimization process can favor it.  The response combines a balancing metric with an enforceable guardrail. Satisfaction provides a second view of performance; content-quality checks constrain what the system may recommend. A release should not be considered successful solely because CTR increases. If satisfaction declines beyond an agreed limit or content violates quality requirements, the change should be blocked or rolled back.  Close by pointing to the two outcome rows: In the weak form, the score improves and the real outcome stays flat—we have lost a signal. In the strong form, the score improves and the real outcome deteriorates—we are causing harm. The lesson is to evaluate whether improving an AI metric still improves the goal it was chosen to represent.  Discussion prompt: “If a new recommender increases CTR by 10%, what additional evidence would you need before calling it an improvement?”  “I’d want evidence that the additional clicks lead to more useful and satisfying experiences. A 10% increase in CTR alone tells us that users clicked more—not that they benefited.”  check: User value: Do users rate the recommendations as more useful or worth their time? Disappointment: Are quick exits, “not interested” responses, complaints, or misleading-content reports increasing? Longer-term outcomes: Do users return voluntarily and remain satisfied over time? Reliability: Does a controlled A/B test show a credible improvement, and do the results hold across important user groups?

## Slide 9
- Goodhart's Law: Weak Form and Strong Form
- “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”
- Weak form: a lost signal
- Recall on an old test set keeps improving while recall in production levels off. Fix: refresh the test set and recalibrate.
- Strong form: real harm
- If the offer generator is rewarded for acceptance rate, it may learn that the biggest discounts get accepted. Acceptance rises while profit margin falls. Fix: add a guardrail
- 9
> Notes: Setup: A call center wants satisfied customers whose problems get solved. That’s the true goal. It’s hard to measure, so management tracks average call time as a proxy and rewards agents for lowering it.  Weak form (the proxy stops being informative): At first, shorter calls really do mean better service. Agents learn the systems, use templates and stop rambling. After a while, though, the easy savings are gone. Call times keep dropping a few seconds each month, but customer satisfaction stays flat. The metric isn’t hurting anything. It just no longer tells you whether service is getting better. The fix is to recalibrate: switch to a measure like first-call resolution.  Strong form (the proxy works against the goal): Management keeps pushing the number down. Agents find the cheapest way to hit it: they rush customers, transfer hard calls, or hang up on them. Average call time looks great. But customers have to call back, complaints rise and satisfaction drops. Pushing the metric harder now makes the real outcome worse. The fix is a guardrail plus a balancing metric: pair call time with repeat-call rate or customer satisfaction, so gaming one shows up in the other.  A simple way to tell them apart: Weak form: the score goes up and the real outcome stays flat. You’ve lost a signal. Strong form: the score goes up and the real outcome goes down. You’re doing harm.   Why can a benchmark that predicted success last year stop predicting it this year, and when does that become harmful?  Goodhart's Law is the central caution in this chapter: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. El-Mhamdi and Hoang separate it into two forms, and the difference affects how urgently we need to respond.  The weak form, on the left of the figure: as we optimize the metric, it stops tracking the real goal. The score keeps rising while the outcome levels off. This wastes effort, but it's recoverable; we can refresh the benchmark or choose a better metric. A NorthStar example: recall on a test set we haven't refreshed since summer keeps improving, while recall on live customers stays flat.  The strong form, on the right: the metric starts moving in the opposite direction from the goal. The score improves while the business gets worse. The opening story was an example. A NorthStar example: if we reward the offer generator for acceptance rate, it may learn that the deepest discounts are accepted most often. Acceptance goes up, and profit margin goes down. The fix is a guardrail, such as a discount cap, plus a balancing metric, sometimes called a counter-metric, such as margin per offer.  Gao, Schulman and Hilton measured the strong form in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), the method used to fine-tune many large language models. As a model was optimized more aggressively against a learned reward, its true quality first improved and then declined. The practical takeaway is that encouraging drift between a benchmark and its goal is expected, so what we control is how quickly we notice it and respond. ## Slide 11
- Five Practices and Three Good Habits
- 11
> Notes: What makes a benchmark a reliable predictor of production performance, rather than a source of false confidence?  The chapter recommends five practices, listed on the left:  - Representative, refreshed test sets. Sample test data from production rather than from development, and refresh it at the pace the world changes. The chapter suggests roughly quarterly for a retail recommender, monthly for customer support, and weekly for fraud detection.  - Time-aware validation. Use only features that would have been known at the moment of prediction, make sure labels don't leak information from the future, and evaluate with the same feature pipeline that production uses.  - Evaluation by segment, also called slice-based evaluation. Report the weakest segment next to the overall score. A model that is 92 percent accurate overall but 60 percent accurate for an important customer group shouldn't be described simply as a 92 percent model.  - Human evaluation baselines. When a large language model (LLM) is used as a judge to score outputs, compare its ratings regularly with human ratings. LLM judges can prefer answers in certain positions, prefer longer answers, or prefer their own outputs.  - Thresholds tied to outcomes. Set the passing score at a level that has predicted acceptable business results in the past.  The navy box lists three habits from the chapter that help prevent the strong form of Goodhart's Law.  The amber box tells a story from this course. On an earlier dataset about eight times smaller, the Platinum loyalty tier had about 33 test customers and roughly 2 churners. Its AUC ranged from 0 to 1 depending only on how the data was split, and at one point a "worse than random" result was reported as a finding. With about 300 customers, Platinum is actually the model's strongest segment. That's why Lab 3 now asks you to report the sample size, n, next to every segment metric. ## Slide 13
- From Promising Offline to Proven Online
- 13
> Notes: After a model wins offline, what does a careful team do before declaring success?   This slide covers the chapter's section on experimentation, which connects benchmark results to business results. Let's go through the four stages from left to right:  - An offline benchmark tells us the model is promising.  - A shadow deployment sends real traffic to the new model without showing its results to users, and a canary release shows its results to a small share of users. ## Slide 14
- Guardrail Metrics Put Risk Tolerance into Numbers
- 14
> Notes: What makes a guardrail dependable, rather than just a number written on a whiteboard?  Guardrails take the level of risk an organization is willing to accept and express it as specific, measurable limits. The chapter lists four properties, shown across the top:  - Specific: we can measure it in production, close to real time. "Performance should be acceptable" is a good goal, but it isn't yet a guardrail.  - Agreed early: governance, legal, product and engineering choose the threshold together before deployment, rather than after a problem.  - Actionable: the response is planned in advance, such as an automatic rollback, a page to the on-call engineer, a human review or a pause. The chapter notes that posting a message in a team chat isn't enough on its own.  - Proportionate: higher-risk systems get more and stricter guardrails. A credit decision model needs more protection than a movie recommender. The risk tier assigned by governance tells us which guardrails are required.  The table shows the chapter's six common guardrail types with example thresholds. Two notes on terms: 99th-percentile (p99) latency is the time within which 99 out of 100 requests finish, and a Severity-1 flag is the most serious category of content problem. Please treat these numbers as examples. A distance threshold that suits one feature may not suit another, so the chapter recommends testing each threshold against your own production history and checking how many alerts it would have produced.  To connect this to the lab: NorthStar's fairness guardrail in Lab 6 allows a recall gap of up to 10 percentage points across loyalty tiers, while the book's retail example uses 8. Each organization's governance board chooses its own number based on its history and risk.  What’s being measured Recall for the churn model is the share of customers who actually churned that the model flagged in advance. If 200 customers left and the model had flagged 160 of them, recall is 80%. Recall across loyalty tiers means recall is calculated separately for each customer group (for example, Bronze, Silver and Gold members). The recall gap is the highest tier’s recall minus the lowest tier’s.  What the threshold means Suppose recall is 84% for Gold members and 76% for Bronze members. The gap is 8 percentage points. Under NorthStar’s Lab 6 rule (up to 10 points), the model passes. Under the book’s retail example (up to 8 points), it’s right at the limit, and at 9 points it would breach. The wording is “percentage points” and not “percent” on purpose. Going from 84% to 76% is a gap of 8 percentage points, but it’s about a 9.5% relative drop. Students often mix these up.  Why it’s a guardrail Retention offers go to the customers the model flags. If the model catches churners well in one tier and poorly in another, the poorly served tier gets fewer save offers. That is unequal treatment, even if overall accuracy looks fine.    Transition: In systems that generate text, guardrails are also code. Here's how NorthStar's offer generator could check each offer.

> Notes: Where should a business rule like a 25 percent discount cap live: in the prompt, in a managed guardrail service, or in our own code?  The most dependable place is our own code, and it's fine for the prompt to mention the rule too. The prompt asks the model to follow the rule; the code check makes sure it's followed.  Let's walk through the three layers in the code:  - Layer 1 is safety. Amazon Bedrock Guardrails, called through its ApplyGuardrail API (application programming interface), checks the text for harmful content, prompt attacks and personally identifiable information (PII), such as email addresses and phone numbers. > Notes: How much unreliability can a system have, and how do we decide what to do once we've used that allowance? - return value > thr    # rises with drift

- return value < thr    # falls with drift

> Notes: If your drift report says no feature has drifted, how can you be confident it's correct?   This is the most important detail to get right in Lab 6, and it ties back to today's theme: a metric is only as reliable as its definition.   Evidently reports two different kinds of numbers depending on the test:  - The Population Stability Index (PSI) is a distance statistic. It gets larger as drift increases, so drift means the value is greater than the threshold.  - The Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test reports a p-value, the probability of seeing a difference this large if nothing had changed. It gets smaller as drift increases, so drift means the value is less than the threshold.   The synthetic table shows this clearly. As we shift the average value of a feature further, the KS p-value drops to zero while PSI rises to 2.3. The two numbers move in opposite directions.   If we loop over both kinds of tests using a single "value greater than threshold" check, the KS results are reversed. A p-value of 0.0 isn't greater than 0.05, so heavily drifted data would be reported as stable. No error message appears, and the report looks clean. Writing a separate comparison for each test, as in the code, avoids the problem.
