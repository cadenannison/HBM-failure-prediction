Every stage has a gate (not a timeline), every artifact is a real document (not a checkbox), every gate has options (not just yes/no).  The Validation Report provides the evidence that the AI system performs adequately; the PRC (Production Readiness Checklist) is the structured gate checklist that determines whether the entire system is ready to move into production—not merely whether the model performs well.  For an AI system, I would expect the PRC to cover areas such as: Performance: accuracy/quality thresholds and acceptance criteria met Safety: harmful or unacceptable behaviors tested and bounded Security: access controls, prompt-injection/adversarial testing, secrets and data protections Privacy & compliance: PII handling, retention, regulatory requirements Responsible AI: bias/fairness and other applicable risk assessments Reliability: failure modes, fallback behavior, resilience, capacity Observability: logging, metrics, tracing, drift/quality monitoring Operations: runbooks, incident response, escalation paths, ownership Cost: expected production consumption and cost limits Rollback: ability to disable, revert, or replace the system safely  So conceptually, I would distinguish the two Stage 6 artifacts this way: Validation Report = “Here is the evidence that the solution works and its risks have been evaluated.” PRC = “Here is the gate checklist demonstrating that the organization is prepared to operate this solution safely in production.” That distinction also explains the transition into Stage 7 – Deploy, whose artifacts are Dashboards + Runbooks. The PRC verifies before deployment that those operational capabilities and controls are actually ready.

## Slide 5
- PHASE 1: PROOF-OF-CONCEPT
- An initial system to test technical feasibility and answer one question: can this approach work at all?
- Purpose
- Demonstrate that the core technical approach is feasible.
- What it is not
- Not a business-value proof, pilot, or prototype.
- Typical scope
- Days to weeks; small team; sample or synthetic data; no production infrastructure.
- Exit criterion
- A clear answer to the technical feasibility question.

## Slide 6
- PHASE 2: PROOF-OF-VALUE
- An initial system focused on proving business value using real or representative data and actual metrics.
- Purpose
- Validate whether the approach produces outcomes that justify continued investment.
- What it is not
- Not production-ready; not for broad scale or robustness.
- Typical scope
- Days to a few weeks; small team; limited infrastructure investment.
- Exit criterion
- Evidence that the value is sufficient to justify building the full system.

## Slide 7
- PHASE 3: PROTOTYPE
- A small end-to-end system in development that assembles the major components of the final solution.
- Purpose
- Demonstrate a complete but non-production version of the system.
- What it is not
- Not production; it lacks hardening for performance, reliability, security, monitoring, and scale.
- Typical scope
- Weeks to months; full team; development environment; end-to-end functionality.
- Exit criterion
- All key functionality demonstrated and confirmed to meet design intent.

## Slide 8
- PHASE 4: PILOT
- The first production deployment to a limited user segment to validate the system in real operating conditions.
- Purpose
- Validate the system with real users in production at controlled scope and risk.
- What it is not
- Not full production; it is a gate to decide whether to scale.
- Typical scope
- Weeks to months; subset of users; full production infrastructure; active monitoring.
- Exit criterion
- Confirmed business value in production, critical issues addressed, and confidence in full rollout.

## Slide 9
- PHASE 5: PRODUCTION
- The full-scale deployment to the complete intended user population, fully operationalized and governed.
- Purpose
- Deliver the system's full value at scale with ongoing operations.
- What it is not
- Not the end of the lifecycle; it begins the monitor stage and ongoing maintenance.
- Typical scope
- Full user population; full production infrastructure; active monitoring and operations.
- Exit criterion
- Production is maintained until retirement, with retraining/redesign decisions driven by review cadence.

- 2. AI System Pipeline Automation
- Pipeline automation is a cornerstone of large-scale production AI systems. Every stage of the AI lifecycle incorporates some type of pipeline automation, including:
- Data Ingestion and Preprocessing Pipelines
- Feature Engineering Pipelines
- Model Training and Validation Pipelines
- Model Deployment and Serving Pipelines
- Monitoring and Feedback Pipelines
- Continuous Integration and Deployment (CI/CD) Pipelines (For AI systems: CI/CT/CD/CM)
- 18
- Just as the moving assembly line was critical for enabling mass production of cars; software pipelines are essential for streamlining the integration of AI systems.
- Different than Workflow Orchestration, which sits at a higher level and may incorporate software and human interactions.
> Notes: End-to-End Machine Learning (ML) Automation refers to the process of automating the entire lifecycle of a machine learning project, from raw data ingestion to model deployment and monitoring. This automation streamlines workflows, improves efficiency, and ensures consistency, allowing organizations to scale their ML efforts effectively.  Key Stages of an End-to-End ML Workflow 1. Data Ingestion and Preprocessing • Ingestion: Automate the extraction of data from various sources (databases, APIs, logs, etc.). • Preprocessing: Automate cleaning, missing value handling, normalization, and feature engineering. • Tools: Airflow, Apache Kafka, Prefect. 2. Feature Engineering • Create and transform features in an automated pipeline. • Use feature stores to reuse, serve, and version features. • Tools: Feature-engine, Feast, Hopsworks. 3. Model Training • Automate hyperparameter tuning and model selection. • Incorporate techniques like AutoML to identify the best algorithms. • Tools: H2O.ai, Google AutoML, AWS SageMaker Autopilot, PyCaret. 4. Model Validation and Evaluation • Automate evaluation metrics (e.g., accuracy, precision, recall) to choose the best model. • Use data validation libraries to ensure data consistency. • Tools: scikit-learn (metrics module), TensorFlow Data Validation, Great Expectations. 5. Model Deployment • Automate deployment to production environments. • Support for batch, real-time, and edge deployment. • Tools: MLflow, Kubeflow, AWS SageMaker, Docker, Kubernetes.  6. Monitoring and Maintenance • Monitor model performance in production for drift or degradation. • Trigger retraining workflows automatically based on performance thresholds. • Tools: Evidently AI, WhyLabs, Arize AI.  Key Components of Automation 1. Pipelines • Define workflows as pipelines to automate the sequential steps. • Examples: TFX (TensorFlow Extended), Apache Airflow, Prefect. 2. AutoML • Automatically selects the best model architecture and hyperparameters. • Examples: Google AutoML, DataRobot, H2O.ai. 3. Infrastructure as Code (IaC) • Automates infrastructure provisioning for data pipelines and ML models. • Examples: Terraform, AWS CloudFormation. 4. Version Control • Manage versions of datasets, features, and models for reproducibility. • Examples: DVC (Data Version Control), MLflow, Pachyderm. 5. CI/CD for ML (MLOps) • Automate testing, validation, and deployment for ML models. • Examples: GitHub Actions, Jenkins, Kubeflow Pipelines. 6. Monitoring and Retraining Triggers • Automate alerts for data drift, model drift, or performance drop. • Examples: Prometheus, Grafana, Evidently AI.  Advantages of ML Automation 1. Scalability: Handles large volumes of data and multiple models efficiently. 2. Consistency: Reduces errors by standardizing processes. 3. Speed: Accelerates development and deployment cycles. 4. Reproducibility: Ensures workflows can be reproduced by other teams or systems. 5. Cost Savings: Reduces manual effort and optimizes resource usage.  Challenges of Automation 1. Complexity: Building and maintaining pipelines require technical expertise. 2. Tool Integration: Ensuring compatibility between various tools. 3. Data Quality: Automated systems depend on high-quality input data. 4. Monitoring: Continuous monitoring to handle unexpected changes or failures.  Example: Automated Customer Churn Prediction  Workflow: 1. Data Pipeline: • Raw customer data is ingested daily using Apache Kafka. • A preprocessing pipeline cleans and aggregates the data. 2. Feature Pipeline: • Features like purchase history, login frequency, and subscription tenure are engineered and stored in Feast. 3. Model Training: • AutoML identifies the best churn prediction model using past data. 4. Model Deployment: • The best model is deployed using MLflow and served via an API for real-time predictions. 5. Monitoring: • Arize AI monitors the model’s performance, triggering retraining when drift is detected.

## Slide 22
- 4. Model Development and Evaluation
- 22
- Model Development: IDE selection (Jupyter, VS Code), Model Platform (SageMaker)
- Model Training: Scalable training infrastructure (e.g., distributed training using TensorFlow, PyTorch, or Spark ML).
- Model Evaluation: The process of measuring how accurately and reliably a trained machine learning model performs on unseen data using predefined performance metrics. (SageMaker)
- Model evaluation for Generative AI and Agentic systems measures how effectively an AI system produces accurate, useful, safe, and reliable outputs while completing tasks under realistic operating conditions. (Bedrock and AgentCore Evaluations)
> Notes: For traditional machine learning, model evaluation primarily measures predictive performance on unseen data. Metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, and RMSE tell us how well a model generalizes beyond its training data.  With Generative AI, however, evaluation becomes much broader because there is often no single “correct” answer. Instead of asking, “Did the model make the right prediction?” we ask, “Did the model produce a response that is useful, accurate, safe, and aligned with the user’s intent?” Evaluation typically considers dimensions such as: Correctness/Factuality – Is the information accurate and free of hallucinations? Relevance – Does the response address the user’s request? Coherence – Is the response logical and well-organized? Completeness – Does it fully answer the question? Groundedness – If using RAG, does the answer align with the retrieved sources? Safety – Does it avoid harmful, biased, or inappropriate content? Latency and Cost – Is the response generated quickly and economically? Evaluation often combines automated metrics, LLM-as-a-judge techniques, and human evaluation, since subjective qualities such as usefulness and writing quality are difficult to capture with traditional metrics alone.  For Agentic AI systems, evaluation expands even further because we are no longer evaluating a single model—we are evaluating the performance of an entire autonomous workflow. An agent must not only generate good text but also reason, plan, use tools, retrieve information, interact with external systems, and complete multi-step tasks. Typical evaluation dimensions include: Task Success – Did the agent accomplish the requested objective? Planning Quality – Did it choose an efficient sequence of actions? Tool Selection – Did it invoke the correct APIs, databases, or software tools? Reasoning Quality – Were intermediate decisions logical and consistent? Robustness – How well does it recover from failures or unexpected situations? Efficiency – How many tool calls, tokens, time, and dollars were required? Safety and Governance – Did it remain within security, privacy, and policy constraints? User Satisfaction – Was the overall experience successful from the user’s perspective? For agentic systems, evaluation frequently resembles system testing more than traditional model benchmarking. Developers measure end-to-end workflows, execution traces, tool usage, and success rates across realistic scenarios rather than relying solely on model-level accuracy.  A concise definition for our course Model evaluation for Generative AI and Agentic systems is the process of measuring how effectively an AI system produces accurate, useful, safe, and reliable outputs while successfully completing real-world tasks under realistic operating conditions. This definition encompasses both Generative AI (evaluating response quality) and Agentic AI (evaluating end-to-end task execution and system behavior).

- 5. Model Deployment and Serving
- 23
- Simple analogy
- Deployment = Installing a restaurant. Serving = Cooking and delivering meals after customers arrive.
- One happens occasionally. The other happens continuously.
- What is the Difference?
> Notes: This distinction has become much more important with GenAI because the “model” is often just one component of a much larger AI application.  Traditional Machine Learning Model Deployment Definition:Model deployment is the process of making a trained model available for production use. Deployment includes activities such as: Packaging the model Creating an API endpoint Configuring infrastructure Setting up security Integrating with applications Automating releases Think of deployment as installing and releasing the model into production.  Model Serving Definition:Model serving is the runtime process of receiving requests, executing model inference, and returning predictions. Serving focuses on: Loading the model into memory Accepting inference requests Scaling inference Managing latency Returning predictions Handling concurrent users Think of serving as running the model after it has been deployed. Simple analogy Deployment = Installing a restaurant. Serving = Cooking and delivering meals after customers arrive. One happens occasionally. The other happens continuously. Traditional Architecture Training      │      ▼ Model Registry      │      ▼ Deploy Model      │      ▼ Serving Endpoint      │      ▼ Application Deployment creates the endpoint. Serving answers prediction requests. How this changes with Generative AI In GenAI, “serving” becomes much richer than simply loading a model into memory. Prompt      │ Prompt Template      │ RAG Retrieval      │ LLM      │ Safety Filters      │ Formatting      │ Response The model is only one stage. Serving now includes an entire inference pipeline. Deployment Deployment now means deploying an entire AI application. Examples include: LLM endpoint Prompt templates Vector database RAG pipeline Guardrails Authentication APIs Monitoring Serving Serving now orchestrates multiple services. A single user request might involve: Retrieve documents from S3 Search a vector database Call Bedrock Invoke moderation filters Re-rank documents Generate citations Stream tokens back to the user Only one of those steps is actually “running the model.” Agentic AI changes the picture again An agent isn’t simply generating text. It is executing work. A request may involve: User Request       │ Planner       │ Reasoning       │ Tool Selection       │ API Calls       │ Database Queries       │ LLM Calls       │ Memory       │ More Planning       │ Final Answer The model is one participant inside a workflow. Agent Deployment Deployment now includes an entire autonomous system. Examples include: Agent framework Planner Memory Tool definitions Permissions Knowledge bases LLM configuration Monitoring Guardrails Human approval workflows You’re deploying an intelligent application—not just a model. Agent Serving Serving becomes workflow orchestration. During one request the serving infrastructure may: Call multiple LLMs Execute Python code Query databases Search the web Invoke APIs Schedule tasks Maintain conversational memory Retry failed actions Ask clarifying questions Generate a final response The serving layer is essentially executing a distributed workflow. A useful way to explain this to students As AI systems have evolved, the distinction between deployment and serving has shifted: Traditional ML: Deployment is making a trained model available in production, while serving is running that deployed model to generate predictions for incoming requests. Generative AI: Deployment means releasing an end-to-end AI application—including the LLM, prompts, retrieval pipeline, guardrails, and APIs—while serving is orchestrating these components to produce grounded, safe, and useful responses. Agentic AI: Deployment involves provisioning an autonomous AI system with its models, tools, memory, permissions, and governance, while serving is executing and coordinating multi-step reasoning, tool use, and decision-making to complete real-world tasks. The key insight is that deployment defines what capabilities are available in production, whereas serving is the runtime execution of those capabilities in response to user requests. As AI systems become more sophisticated, deployment expands from a single model to an entire intelligent platform, and serving evolves from simple inference into orchestration of complex, multi-step workflows.

> Notes: Idempotency Definition: An operation is idempotent if executing it multiple times produces the same result as executing it exactly once.  Pronounced: eye-DEM-po-tent, with the stress on DEM.   In other words, “If I accidentally repeat this request, nothing bad happens.” Why is this important? Cloud systems are unreliable by nature. Network packets are lost. Timeouts occur. Lambda functions retry. API Gateway retries. Message queues redeliver messages. Users press the button twice. Without idempotency, duplicate work can occur.  Dead-Letter Handling A dead-letter queue (DLQ) is a place where failed work goes after repeated retries. Think of it as the system’s “hospital.”  What is the difference between pipeline automation and orchestration? Pipeline automation makes individual AI workflows repeatable and automatic; orchestration coordinates those workflows, their dependencies, triggers, decisions, and failure handling across the end-to-end AI lifecycle.  An AI example Suppose you’re building a churn-prediction system. A training pipeline might automate:  Extract data → Validate → Transform → Train → Evaluate → Register model Instead of an engineer manually running those six steps, the pipeline runs them automatically and in the correct order. Orchestration sits at a higher level and might coordinate: New data arrives → Run data pipeline → Check data quality → Trigger training pipeline → Evaluate model → If approved, trigger deployment pipeline → Run canary test → Promote or rollback → Notify team

## Slide 30
- 9. Enterprise AI Organization Operating Models
> Notes: Federated and Governed Self-Service both distribute AI development to business/domain teams. The difference is where the operational burden lives and how governance is enforced. A useful shorthand is: Federated = “Here is the common platform and the rules; domain AI teams still engineer and operate their solutions.”Governed Self-Service = “Here are paved roads that automatically apply the rules; product teams consume them and concentrate on the AI solution.”   The biggest difference is therefore not centralization versus decentralization. Both are decentralized in terms of who builds AI. The distinction is how much complexity the central platform absorbs. 1. In Federated, domain AI teams are still engineering teams Look at column 2. The central platform team provides things such as security, a model gateway, CI/CD templates, model registry, and monitoring/observability. But underneath it you still have dedicated organizations such as Finance AI Team, Marketing AI Team, and Supply Chain AI Team. The figure explicitly says these teams own their: apps, data, features, outcomes. That means the central organization provides reusable building blocks, while each domain team assembles those components into a working AI system. For example, suppose Finance wants an accounts-payable copilot. Under Federated, the Finance AI team might choose the model, construct RAG, integrate Finance data, configure the model gateway, create evaluation tests, configure CI/CD, establish monitoring, and deploy/operate the application—all while complying with enterprise standards. So: Central platform → capabilities → domain AI engineers → AI application Governance is partly technological and partly organizational.  2. Governed Self-Service pushes another layer of responsibility into the platform Column 4 is fundamentally more ambitious. Notice what the platform exposes: Models & RAG → Agents → Data Services → Evaluation & Testing → Deployment APIs And beneath those capabilities is an explicit layer of: AUTOMATED GUARDRAILS — IAM • Policy • Security • Evaluation • Monitoring • FinOps That is the architectural clue. The organization isn’t merely sharing infrastructure. It is turning AI engineering into a set of consumable platform services with policy built into them. The Finance team shouldn’t need to become an expert in IAM implementation, model routing, observability infrastructure, evaluation pipelines, deployment machinery, or cost controls every time it creates an application. Instead: AI platform → governed paved roads → product team → AI application The platform becomes an internal AI product.  The one-question test If you’re trying to classify a real enterprise, ask: “When a business team wants to put a new AI application into production, how much platform engineering does that team itself have to understand and perform?” If the answer is “quite a bit, but they use standardized enterprise components”, you’re probably looking at Federated. If the answer is “very little—the platform exposes self-service workflows/APIs and automatically handles most security, governance, evaluation, deployment, monitoring, and cost controls”, you’re approaching Governed Self-Service. That distinction also explains why the figure positions Governed Self-Service as especially appropriate for organizations with mature platform teams and a high volume of AI use cases: the economics become compelling when the substantial investment in those paved roads can be amortized across dozens or hundreds of AI products.
