## Slide 2
- Airbnb Zipline: The case for feature stores
> Notes: Presentation Notes — Airbnb Zipline: The Case for Feature Stores  Opening “This slide illustrates why feature stores emerged in large ML organizations. The problem wasn’t simply storing features—it was that every team was independently rebuilding the same data and feature infrastructure.” Before Zipline: fragmented ML pipelines “Look at the top half. We have six teams solving different business problems—fraud, churn, recommendations, analytics, risk, and operations. Each team builds its own pipeline: Raw data → ETL → features → model That seems reasonable when there are only one or two teams. But as the organization scales, several problems appear.” First, duplicated engineering. “Different teams repeatedly implement similar features—customer activity, transaction history, location, engagement, and so forth.” Second, inconsistent definitions. “One team might define customer_activity_30d one way while another defines it slightly differently. Now we have multiple versions of what should conceptually be the same feature.” Third, training/serving skew. “Independent pipelines increase the chance that training and production compute features differently.” Fourth, debugging becomes cross-team detective work. “When something breaks, you have to determine which pipeline, transformation, dataset, or feature definition caused the problem.” After Zipline: shared feature infrastructure “The lower half shows the architectural change. Instead of six teams independently rebuilding the entire stack, Zipline provides a shared data and ML platform. Teams can publish, discover, and reuse common feature definitions rather than recreating them.” Conceptually, we move from: Six teams × six independent pipelines to: Shared platform → reusable features → many ML consumers “This is a classic platform-engineering tradeoff: centralize the capabilities that benefit from consistency while allowing teams to remain independent in the models and business problems they’re solving.”  Why feature reuse matters “Feature reuse is more important than it may initially sound. Suppose the fraud team already created a trustworthy feature: transactions_last_30d The churn team shouldn’t necessarily have to rebuild it. With a shared feature platform, that feature can become a reusable organizational asset. And because multiple models consume the same definition, we also improve consistency.”  Interpret the metrics carefully “The numbers on the right illustrate the intended organizational benefits: shorter time to production, greater feature reuse, and faster debugging. I would treat those specific numbers as illustrative unless we have a primary Airbnb source supporting those exact measurements. The important architectural point doesn’t depend on the precise percentages.” The broader pattern is: Less duplicated infrastructure → more reuse → greater consistency → faster development and debugging.  Connection to SageMaker Feature Store “This is directly analogous to what we’ve been discussing with SageMaker Feature Store. The technologies differ, but the architectural motivation is similar: Define features consistently.Make them discoverable and reusable.Support both training and serving.Maintain lineage and governance.  So when we use a feature store in our AWS architecture, we’re addressing problems that become very real once multiple teams and models depend upon the same data.”  The deeper lesson “There’s an important scaling lesson here. A feature store may look like unnecessary infrastructure when an organization has: one team + one model. But consider: 20 teams + 100 models + thousands of features. At that scale, duplicated feature logic becomes an organizational problem, not merely a coding inconvenience.” You can summarize the progression as: One model: feature engineering is code.Many models: features become shared assets.Many teams: feature management becomes platform infrastructure.  Closing takeaway “The lesson from Zipline isn’t simply ‘Airbnb built a feature store.’ The deeper lesson is: As ML organizations scale, the unit of reuse shouldn’t just be the model—it should also be the feature. A shared feature platform turns features from isolated calculations buried inside individual pipelines into governed, reusable, consistent data products that many models can consume. And that is ultimately the business case for investing in feature-store infrastructure.”   Before Zipline (Airbnb's feature store, 2017): Each ML team computed its own features independently Same feature ("days since host registration") computed 6 different ways across 6 teams Training/serving skew: features computed differently in notebooks vs. production Feature reuse: 0% — every team rebuilt from scratch Debugging time: "weeks of forensic work" when production models underperformed  After Zipline: Central feature computation platform; features are published, versioned, and shared Same feature computation code runs for both training and serving — architecturally guaranteed Airbnb ML engineers spend 80% less time on feature engineering (per Airbnb Engineering Blog) Feature reuse: 60% of features used by multiple teams Time to production for new models: 2-3 weeks vs. 3-4 months  The Zipline Lessons for NorthStar: Feature standardization pays dividends immediately across multiple models The online/offline store architecture is the only way to guarantee training/serving consistency Feature reuse is a force multiplier: features built for churn prediction can be reused for offer personalization  Notes: "Airbnb is the canonical case study for why feature stores exist. The 2017 Zipline paper essentially defined the feature store pattern that SageMaker Feature Store, Feast, Tecton, and Hopsworks implement today. The lesson is not 'Airbnb is smart' — it's 'the same pain that forced Airbnb to build Zipline is the same pain you're preventing in NorthStar by using SageMaker Feature Store.'"

## Slide 3
- Training/serving Skew: The root causes
> Notes: Brief Presentation Notes — Training/Serving Skew: Root Causes Opening “Training/serving skew occurs when the model sees different feature values, logic, or context in production than it saw during training. A model can be excellent in development and still perform poorly in production if these two environments aren’t consistent.” Four root causes 1. Code divergence “The training system and serving system use different versions of code or libraries. For example, training uses feature logic version 1.0 while production has moved to 1.2. Same feature name—but potentially different calculation.” 2. Data freshness “Training and serving may operate against different snapshots of the underlying data. If training uses an older snapshot while production uses newer or differently refreshed data, feature distributions can diverge.” 3. Preprocessing inconsistency “This is one of the most common problems. Training might normalize, filter, encode, or aggregate data one way while the production pipeline implements the transformation slightly differently. For example, training calculates avg_spend_30d over 30 calendar days while serving calculates it over four weeks.” Why Feature Stores help “The first three problems have a common solution: centralize feature definitions and feature computation. That’s one of the major motivations for a feature store. Rather than maintaining separate implementations for training and serving, we establish a common source of truth for feature definitions and values.” 4. Temporal leakage — a different problem “The fourth problem is different: temporal leakage. Suppose we’re constructing a training example representing what we knew at time T. If the feature calculation accidentally incorporates information from T+1, we’re allowing the model to learn from the future. That can produce excellent validation results that disappear in production because the production system doesn’t actually have future information.” A simple rule for students is: For a prediction at time T, every feature must be computable using only information available at or before T. Why a Feature Store isn’t enough “Notice that the slide separates temporal leakage from the other three causes. A feature store can help substantially with consistency and point-in-time retrieval, but it doesn’t automatically prevent every form of leakage. We still need correct feature definitions, point-in-time joins, testing, and human code review.” Takeaway “The central lesson is: Production ML requires consistency across code, data, transformations, and time. Feature stores address much of the training/serving consistency problem, but good ML engineering still requires us to reason carefully about what information was actually available when the prediction would have been made.”   What is training/serving skew? The model receives different input distributions at serving time than it was trained on — causing silent performance degradation.  Root Cause 1: Different computation code Training: feature computed in a Pandas notebook (custom logic) Serving: feature computed by a different function in the inference handler Even a subtle difference (float rounding, null handling) causes skew  Root Cause 2: Different data freshness Training: features computed from the full historical dataset Serving: features computed from a real-time snapshot that may be stale or missing records  Root Cause 3: Different preprocessing steps Training: outliers clipped at the 99th percentile of the training data Serving: same clipping threshold applied to production data where the 99th percentile has shifted  Root Cause 4: Temporal leakage Training features inadvertently use information from after the label was assigned. This is the insidious one. The Feature Store doesn't protect you from it. Temporal leakage happens in your feature design, not your pipeline architecture. This is why feature definitions need careful review against: 'Is this information available at the moment the model would make this prediction in production?'  Prevention: The Feature Store — single computation, written once, read by both training and serving.

## Slide 5
- Data governance Framework for AI
> Notes: Presentation Notes — Data Governance Framework for AI Opening “This slide shifts our focus from building data pipelines to governing the data that flows through them. For AI systems, governance means being able to answer four fundamental questions: Who can access the data? What kind of data is it? What happened to it? And can we trust it? The pyramid organizes those questions from foundational controls at the bottom to measurable data reliability at the top.” 1. Access Control — Who can use the data? “At the foundation is access control: the right people should have access to the right data at the right time—and everyone else should not. In AWS, this can involve IAM roles and policies, fine-grained permissions, and private network access. For example, a data scientist might be allowed to read approved customer features but not the raw personally identifiable information used to construct them.” Key point: governance begins by controlling who can do what with which data.  2. Data Classification — What kind of data is this? “Once we control access, we need to understand what we’re protecting. Not all data carries the same risk. We might classify data as public, internal, confidential, or restricted. We also need to identify things such as PII—personally identifiable information. AWS services such as Amazon Macie can help discover and identify sensitive information stored in S3.” A useful question for students is: “Can we govern data effectively if we don’t know which datasets contain sensitive information?”  3. Audit Trail — What happened? “The third layer is the audit trail. We want to be able to reconstruct what happened: Who accessed the data?What changed?When did it change?Which dataset or version was used? AWS services contribute different pieces here. CloudTrail records AWS API activity, CloudWatch provides operational logs and metrics, and catalog/lineage metadata helps us understand how datasets relate to one another. For AI, this becomes particularly important when we need to trace a model result back through its features and source data.” Conceptually: Prediction → model → features → transformation → source data  4. Data Quality SLAs — Can we trust the data? “At the top is measurable data quality. Instead of saying, ‘We expect good data,’ we define explicit targets. For NorthStar, that might mean: Freshness under 24 hoursCompleteness of at least 99%Validation accuracy or conformance above a defined threshold Now data quality becomes something we can measure, monitor, alert on, and improve.” This connects directly to the earlier data-contract discussion: The contract defines the quality promise; governance ensures that promise is owned, monitored, and auditable.  Why the pyramid is ordered this way “The ordering is intentional. There’s limited value in having extremely accurate data if unauthorized users can access it. Likewise, access controls alone aren’t enough if we don’t know which datasets contain sensitive information. So governance builds upward: Control access → classify data → establish traceability → measure trustworthiness.”  Connection to AI “For AI systems, these controls become especially important because models inherit characteristics of their data. If we can’t answer: Where did the training data come from?Who had access to it?Does it contain sensitive information?Which version trained this model?Did it satisfy our quality requirements? then we have a governance problem before we even begin evaluating the model itself.” Closing takeaway “The central idea is that data governance isn’t a separate compliance activity sitting outside the ML pipeline. It is part of the engineering architecture. For NorthStar, we want every important dataset and feature to have: controlled access, known classification, traceable history, and measurable quality. Those capabilities give us the foundation for trustworthy, reproducible, and governable AI systems.”   Data Governance for AI — four layers:  Layer 1: Access Control (who can read/write which data) IAM roles scoped to specific S3 prefixes and Feature Groups No individual access keys; all access through role assumption NorthStar: three roles, each scoped to their functional domain  Layer 2: Data Classification (sensitivity level drives handling rules) PII (customers.csv): masked before Feature Store ingest; never used as direct feature Financial (transactions): aggregated only; raw amounts excluded from features Internal (store_events, product_catalog): standard access controls  Layer 3: Audit Trail (record of who accessed what, when) S3 access logging enabled → CloudTrail → CloudWatch Logs SageMaker Model Registry: approval chain recorded with timestamp and approver identity Glue Data Catalog: change history tracked  Layer 4: Data Quality SLAs (contractual guarantees on pipeline outputs) Data contracts (Lab 2 deliverable) define quality obligations Violations trigger automated alerts and pipeline pauses  Notes: "Data governance is the set of rules that let an organization trust its data — including the data that feeds its AI systems. Without governance, you cannot answer the regulatory questions that matter: 'Who trained on this customer's data?' 'Who approved this model?' 'When was this feature last validated?' These questions arise in enterprise contexts, regulatory audits, and, increasingly, court cases involving AI systems.

## Slide 6
- Privacy engineering for AI
> Notes: Presentation Notes — Privacy Engineering for AI Opening “This slide distinguishes privacy engineering from simply having a privacy policy. Privacy engineering means designing the data and ML pipeline so that sensitive information is removed, transformed, or tightly controlled before it reaches components that don’t need it.” Start with the raw data “On the left, our source data contains fields such as name, email, customer ID, age, and spending. Some of these fields are directly identifying. Others may become identifying when combined with additional information. The first principle is data minimization: If the ML model doesn’t need a piece of personal information, don’t propagate it through the ML pipeline.” Step 1 — PII masking “The first engineering control is to remove or transform identifying information. Here, name and email are removed, while customer_id is replaced with a pseudonymous or hashed identifier. We preserve the ability to associate records with the same entity when necessary, but downstream systems don’t need the person’s actual identity.” A useful distinction: Masking removes or obscures information. Pseudonymization replaces an identity with another identifier. Neither should automatically be treated as true anonymization. “If the hashed identifier can still be linked back to a person, the data may still require privacy protections.” Step 2 — Feature engineering “Feature engineering now operates primarily on information relevant to the prediction problem. Instead of carrying a customer’s name or email, we create features such as: total_purchases_90davg_order_valueagespend The question we should continually ask is: Does this feature actually need personal information to accomplish the modeling objective? If not, don’t include it.” Step 3 — Model training “By the time we reach the training matrix, directly identifying fields have been excluded. This reduces the amount of sensitive information exposed to notebooks, training jobs, model artifacts, debugging processes, and downstream users. That’s an example of privacy by design: we reduce unnecessary exposure through the architecture itself rather than relying exclusively on people to remember privacy rules.” Explain the red boxes “The red boxes show what we’re trying to prevent. Without this boundary, fields such as name and email might simply propagate: Source → features → training → model Instead, we’re deliberately stopping unnecessary PII near the beginning of the pipeline: PII enters → classify → minimize/mask/pseudonymize → engineer features → train The earlier we reduce unnecessary sensitive information, the smaller our downstream privacy exposure.” Connect to governance “This connects directly to the previous governance slide. Governance tells us: What data is sensitive and who may access it? Privacy engineering asks: How should we design the system so that sensitive data isn’t present where it isn’t needed in the first place? Those are complementary controls.” Important clarification about “GDPR Compliant” “I would be careful with the green ‘GDPR Compliant’ label on this slide. Removing PII from a training matrix does not by itself establish GDPR compliance. GDPR involves broader requirements around lawful processing, purpose limitation, data minimization, security, retention, individual rights, accountability, and other obligations. So I would describe this architecture as: ‘Privacy-by-design controls that can support GDPR compliance.’ That’s more accurate than implying that masking three fields makes the entire system compliant.” AWS connection “In an AWS implementation, several services can contribute to this architecture. Amazon Macie can help discover sensitive data in S3. IAM and Lake Formation can restrict who is allowed to access sensitive datasets. KMS can protect data through encryption. Glue transformations or other processing jobs can perform masking, tokenization, or pseudonymization before data reaches downstream feature and ML pipelines. And CloudTrail can contribute an audit record of AWS API activity.” Closing takeaway “The main principle is: The safest sensitive data is often the sensitive data you never propagate downstream. For an AI pipeline, privacy engineering means identifying sensitive information early, minimizing what we retain, transforming what we still need, restricting access, and ensuring that the model receives only the information necessary for its intended purpose. That’s the difference between treating privacy as a policy document and treating privacy as an engineering requirement.”   Privacy Threats Specific to AI Systems:  1. PII in Features: Including names, emails, or exact addresses in features exposes them to model inversion attacks.Control: Pseudonymization — use customer_id as the join key; never use PII as a feature.  2. Membership Inference: An attacker can determine whether a specific individual was in the training dataset by probing the model.Control: Differential privacy training (DP-SGD) for high-risk models; record-level data access logging.  3. Model Inversion: With sufficient queries, an attacker can reconstruct training data from model outputs.Control: Rate limiting on inference endpoints; output perturbation for sensitive attribute predictions.  4. Regulatory Compliance (GDPR Article 17 — Right to Erasure): If a customer requests deletion, can you remove them from training data and retrain? Control: Data lineage tracking (which training runs used this customer) + model retraining infrastructure.  NorthStar PII handling: customers.csv → mask name, email, phone, address before any downstream processing Feature Store contains only: customer_id (hashed), derived numeric features Training data: no PII; only customer_id as identifier  Notes: "GDPR and CCPA are not hypothetical. NorthStar's customers include EU residents. Any EU customer has the right to request deletion of their data — including removal from model training. Without data lineage and model retraining infrastructure, you cannot comply. This is a real operational requirement, not an academic exercise." We cover the full regulatory framework in L18-L19 (Security/Privacy). Today: understand that privacy constraints shape data engineering choices.

## Slide 7
- Data lineage: building the audit trail
> Notes: Presentation Notes — Data Lineage: Building the Audit Trail Opening “Data lineage answers a deceptively simple question: Where did this data—or this prediction—come from? In a production AI system, we want to trace the complete path from the original source data through every transformation, feature, model version, and ultimately the deployed endpoint.” Walk down the lineage chain “Start at the top with transactions.parquet in our raw S3 data lake. That data passes through the ingest_transactions Glue ETL job, producing a cleaned transaction dataset. A second Glue job, build_features, transforms those transactions into customer_features. Those features are used to train the churn_model, which is ultimately deployed to our northstar-churn-prod SageMaker endpoint.” So the lineage is: Raw transactions → ETL → cleaned transactions → feature engineering → customer features → model → production endpoint Lineage is more than drawing arrows “The arrows are useful, but real lineage requires metadata about every step. Notice that we’re capturing things such as: dataset location and schema, record counts, job and code versions, execution timestamps, data hashes, feature versions, model versions and metrics, deployment information. That metadata turns a pipeline diagram into an auditable history.” Why this matters for debugging “Suppose our churn model suddenly behaves differently on Monday. Rather than asking, ‘What changed?’ and manually searching through multiple systems, lineage lets us work backward: Which model version is deployed?Which features trained it?Which feature job generated those features?Which processed dataset did that job use?Which raw transaction data produced that dataset? Good lineage dramatically reduces the time required for root-cause analysis.” Impact analysis works in the other direction “Lineage is also valuable going forward. Suppose someone proposes changing the definition of transaction_amount. Before making that change, we’d like to ask: What downstream assets depend on this field? The lineage graph might reveal: transaction_amount → customer_features → churn_model → production endpoint Now we understand the blast radius before making the change.” Reproducibility “Lineage also supports reproducibility. If someone asks six months from now: ‘Can you reproduce model version 1.0?’ we need more than the model artifact. We need to know: which source-data version,which transformation code,which feature definitions,which training dataset,and which model configuration produced that artifact.” A useful principle is: A model is reproducible only if its data and transformation history are reproducible. Connection to governance “This is why lineage appeared in our governance framework under audit trail. Governance asks us to prove what happened. Lineage provides much of that evidence. It supports debugging and reproducibility, but it can also support compliance investigations, change management, and accountability.” Connect to data contracts “There’s also an important connection to data contracts. Contracts tell us what should happen. Lineage tells us what actually happened and what depended on it. Together they give us both sides of trustworthy data engineering: Expectation + evidence.”  The “under 10 seconds” idea “The green callout captures the operational goal. We don’t want lineage information scattered across notebooks, people’s memories, Glue logs, S3 folders, and Git repositories. Ideally, an engineer should be able to select a production model or feature and quickly reconstruct its upstream dependency chain.” Closing takeaway “The main idea is that lineage transforms our pipeline from a collection of jobs into a traceable system. For every important model or prediction, we want to be able to answer: What data produced it?What transformations touched that data?Which feature and model versions were involved?When did each step occur?  And in the opposite direction: If this dataset changes, what downstream features and models could be affected? That’s why data lineage is not merely documentation—it is part of the operational infrastructure for reliable and governable AI.”   Complete NorthStar Lineage Chain (churn model): Source:   customers.csv (raw, version: 2026-09-17, hash: a3f2...)   transactions.parquet (raw, 2026-09-17, hash: b7c1...)  ↓ Glue Job: customer-feature-extraction (v2.3, run: 2026-09-18 02:14 UTC)   → Output: s3://northstar-processed/customers/2026-09-18/*.parquet  ↓ Glue Job: transaction-aggregation (v1.8, run: 2026-09-18 02:47 UTC)     → Output: northstar-dev-customer-features Feature Group (ingested 02:53 UTC)  ↓ SageMaker Training Job: churn-xgb-v12 (instance: ml.m5.xlarge, 2026-09-18 03:15 UTC)   → Training data: northstar-dev-customer-features, latest record per customer   → Artifact: s3://northstar-artifacts/churn/v12/model.tar.gz  ↓ Model Registry: churn-model-v12 (approved: 2026-09-19 14:22 UTC, approver: governance-role)  ↓ Endpoint: northstar-churn-prod (deployed: 2026-09-20 09:00 UTC)  Lab 2 deliverable: Document this lineage chain for your own pipeline. Check the Glue job version. Glue job output looks right, but model is wrong? Check the training data snapshot. In 5 minutes you know where the problem is." Without lineage, the same investigation takes days or weeks.

## Slide 8
- Data contracts in practice
> Notes: Presentation Notes — Data Contracts in Practice Opening “This slide moves data contracts from a document or specification into an operational lifecycle. The key idea is that a data contract isn’t something we write once and forget. It is continuously defined, enforced, monitored, and versioned as the data system evolves.” 1. Define — establish the agreement “We begin by defining what the producer promises to its consumers. That includes: schema and datatypes, semantic meaning, quality requirements, freshness or SLA expectations, and access requirements. For example, our producer might promise that customer_id is always present, transaction amounts are nonnegative, and new transaction data arrives within two hours.” The important point is: Make assumptions explicit before consumers build dependencies on them. Consumer agreement “The next step is agreement between producer and consumer. The producer shouldn’t independently decide what constitutes acceptable data. Consumers have requirements too. So the contract represents a shared interface between teams. The ‘signature’ here is conceptual—it might actually be implemented through a pull request, code review, approval workflow, or governance process.” 2. Enforce — turn the contract into executable controls “This is where the contract becomes operational. Instead of merely documenting: customer_id cannot be null, our pipeline actually tests it. AWS Glue jobs, Glue Data Quality, schema validation, IAM policies, or custom checks can enforce different parts of the contract.” A useful distinction is: Contract = promise.Validation = test of the promise.Enforcement = what the system does when the promise is violated.  3. Monitor — verify behavior over time “Passing validation once isn’t enough. We also monitor the data continuously: Is completeness deteriorating?Is freshness getting worse?Are schemas changing?Are SLA violations increasing? This connects directly to our observability discussion. A contract gives our monitoring system something very valuable: explicit thresholds for what normal and acceptable mean.” 4. Version — manage change safely “Eventually, the data will change. Maybe we add a new optional field. That’s potentially backward-compatible. Or perhaps we change a field’s datatype, units, or semantic meaning. That may be a breaking change. Rather than surprising downstream consumers, we version the contract and coordinate the transition.” For example: v1.2 → v1.3 for a compatible additionv1.x → v2.0 for a breaking change “The exact numbering convention is less important than managing change deliberately.” The lifecycle loops back “Notice the large arrow returning to Define. That’s important. Monitoring may tell us that an SLA is unrealistic. A consumer may need a new field. A business definition may change. So: Define → Enforce → Monitor → Version → Define again Data contracts are therefore a continuous lifecycle, not a one-time artifact.” What happens when the contract is violated? “The red path at the bottom shows the operational consequence. Suppose a Glue job discovers that incoming data violates a critical contract rule. Instead of allowing bad data to propagate: Validation fails → pipeline stops or quarantines the data → alert is generated → team investigates → problem is corrected → processing resumes. This is the behavior we want.” Why stopping can be better than succeeding “This introduces an important production-engineering principle: Sometimes a failed pipeline is safer than a successful pipeline producing bad data. If incorrect transaction data propagates into our features, the feature pipeline may succeed, the model endpoint may succeed, and predictions may still be returned—but those predictions could be wrong. A controlled failure is visible. Silent corruption is much harder to detect.” Connect to lineage “This also connects to the lineage slide we just discussed. When a violation occurs, lineage helps answer: Where did this data originate?Which transformation produced it?What downstream features depend on it?Which models could be affected? So contracts and lineage complement each other: Contracts define expectations.Monitoring detects violations.Lineage shows impact and provenance.”  Closing takeaway “The main lesson from this slide is that a mature data contract is not merely YAML sitting in a repository. It becomes part of the operating behavior of the data platform: Define the promise → agree on it → enforce it automatically → monitor it continuously → version it safely → respond when it is violated. That’s how we move from documented expectations to reliable data engineering controls—and ultimately protect the features and models that depend on that data.”   The Data Contract Implementation Pattern:  Step 1: Define the contract (schema + quality + SLA) Document schema for each field: type, nullable, allowed values/ranges Define quality constraints: null_rate_max, duplicate_rate_max, value_range Define SLA: data freshness, delivery time, success rate  Step 2: Enforce the contract at the pipeline boundary # In Glue job — validate before writing to processed zone def validate_contract(df, contract):     for field, constraints in contract['schema'].items():         null_rate = df[field].isna().mean()         assert null_rate <= constraints['null_rate_max'], \             f"NULL rate {null_rate:.3%} exceeds contract {constraints['null_rate_max']:.3%}”  Step 3: Monitor contract compliance over time Emit compliance metrics to CloudWatch Alert on contract violations Version contracts with the data pipeline — breaking changes require migration  Step 4: Consumer acknowledgment Downstream teams sign the contract — they know what to expect Schema-breaking changes require advance notice and a consumer migration window  Notes: "Data contracts are the handshake between data producers and consumers. Without them, the ML team discovers that the data team changed a field name during a post-mortem following the model's failure in production. With them, the change requires a migration window, a version bump, and consumer acknowledgment before any data changes."

## Slide 9
- Data pipeline testing
> Notes: Presentation Notes — Data Pipeline Testing Opening “This slide applies the familiar software testing pyramid to data engineering. The basic principle is simple: Run many small, fast tests continuously, and use fewer expensive end-to-end tests as we move up the pyramid. We don’t want to discover a simple transformation error after running a 60-minute production-scale pipeline.” 1. Unit Tests — test individual transformations “At the bottom are unit tests. These should be our most numerous, fastest, and cheapest tests. Suppose we have a function that calculates: days_since_last_transaction or converts transaction amounts into a standard currency. We can test that logic independently with small, controlled inputs.” For example: Given these five transactions, does avg_amount_30d produce exactly the expected value? “These tests should run on essentially every code change and provide immediate feedback to developers.”  2. Integration Tests — do the components work together? “Next are integration tests.  Now we’re testing interactions between components rather than a single function. For example: Can the Glue job read the expected data from S3?Does the transformed output have the expected structure?Can the resulting features be written to Feature Store?Do our data-quality rules execute correctly? These tests are slower, but they catch problems that unit tests cannot—particularly problems at system boundaries.” 3. Contract Tests — are producer and consumer still compatible? “The third level is particularly important given our earlier discussion of data contracts. Contract tests ask: Did an upstream change violate something a downstream consumer depends upon? We might test: required fields, datatypes, semantic constraints, allowed values, quality thresholds, schema compatibility. If the producer removes customer_id or changes amount from dollars to cents, we want to detect that before the change reaches production.” A useful connection is: The data contract defines the promise; the contract test verifies compatibility with that promise.  4. Volume Tests — does it still work at production scale? “At the top are volume tests.  A pipeline that works perfectly with 1,000 records may behave very differently with 500 million. Here we’re testing things such as: processing time, scalability, memory usage, partitioning behavior, and bottlenecks. These tests are expensive, so we run relatively few of them and less frequently—perhaps before a major release or significant architecture change.”  Why the pyramid shape matters “The width of the pyramid represents where most of our testing effort should occur. At the bottom: Many tests → fast → cheap → narrow scope As we move upward: Fewer tests → slower → expensive → broader scope The goal is to detect problems at the lowest and cheapest possible level.” For example: “If a feature calculation is wrong, I’d much rather discover it in a five-second unit test than after running a 45-minute Glue job over millions of records.”  Connect this to CI/CD “This testing pyramid becomes particularly powerful when integrated into CI/CD. A proposed pipeline change might move through: Code commit→ Unit tests→ Integration tests→ Contract tests→ Deploy→ Production monitoring  If a test fails, we stop the change before bad data propagates downstream.” Testing and observability are complementary “One distinction is important. Testing asks: Does the system behave correctly under conditions we’ve anticipated? Observability asks: What is the system actually doing in production? We need both. Tests prevent known classes of problems. Monitoring and observability help us discover behaviors we didn’t anticipate.” Connect to AI “Why does this matter for machine learning? Because a data pipeline can run successfully while silently producing incorrect features. And once incorrect features reach the model, the model may still return perfectly valid-looking predictions. So the dependency chain is: Pipeline correctness → feature correctness → model reliability. Testing the data system is therefore part of testing the AI system.” Closing takeaway “The key principle is: Catch failures as early, cheaply, and locally as possible. Use unit tests for transformation logic, integration tests for component interactions, contract tests for producer-consumer compatibility, and volume tests for production-scale behavior. The result is the message at the bottom of the slide: Higher confidence → more reliable data → better AI.”    AI Data Pipeline Testing Hierarchy:  Unit Tests (per transformation function): Input: known DataFrame with controlled values Expected output: explicitly specified Examples: test that days_since_last_purchase returns 0 when the last purchase falls on the feature cutoff T, NaN when no transactions  Integration Tests (per pipeline): Run the complete Glue job on a 1,000-row sample Validate output schema, row count, and quality metrics Must complete in < 5 minutes (fast feedback loop)  Data Contract Tests (at pipeline boundary): Validate that pipeline output conforms to the signed data contract Run automatically on every pipeline execution Contract violations halt the pipeline  Volume/Performance Tests (monthly or pre-release): Run the pipeline on the full dataset (4.5M transaction records) Validate that it completes within the SLA window  Notes: "Data pipeline testing is the most commonly skipped engineering practice in AI projects. Teams test their model code (sometimes) but not their data transformation code (rarely). A bug in a feature computation function that passes all unit tests but fails on the actual production data distribution is exactly the kind of failure that causes production incidents." In Lab 2, students are required to include at least one unit test for each feature computation function and a contract test.

## Slide 12
- The data engineering maturity arc
> Notes: Presentation Notes — The Data Engineering Maturity Arc Opening “This slide brings together many of the concepts we’ve discussed into a maturity progression. The question isn’t simply, ‘Does our data pipeline run?’ As systems mature, the question becomes: Can we see what’s happening, and eventually can the system respond safely to problems on its own? The progression is Build → Observe → Automate.” Stage 1 — Basic Pipeline: “It works.” “At the first level, our objective is straightforward: deliver business value. We have: Data sources → ETL/ELT processing → analytics or AI The pipeline runs, produces the expected output, and supports the application. That’s necessary—but operationally we’re still fairly reactive. If something goes wrong, someone may have to discover the problem and manually investigate it.” Key idea: Stage 1 answers:  Can we make it work?  Stage 2 — Observable Pipeline: “We can see it.” “The second stage adds observability. Now we’re monitoring things such as: pipeline failures, latency, data quality, freshness, volume, and cost. Instead of waiting for a downstream user to tell us something looks wrong, the system generates metrics and alerts.” This connects directly to the observability material from earlier: “A job completing successfully doesn’t necessarily mean the pipeline is healthy. We also need to know whether the data is complete, timely, and behaving normally.” Key idea: Stage 2 answers:  Do we know when something is going wrong—and can we diagnose why?  Stage 3 — Autonomous Pipeline: “It heals itself.” “The third stage closes the loop. Instead of: Detect → alert a human → wait for intervention we begin automating appropriate responses: Detect → Diagnose → Remediate → Verify For example, the system might: retry a failed job, scale resources when volume spikes, reprocess a failed partition, roll back a problematic deployment, or quarantine bad data.” The critical word is verify. “Automation shouldn’t simply take an action. It should determine whether that action actually restored the system to a healthy state.”  A concrete example “Imagine our Glue pipeline normally processes five million transactions each night. Tonight, a job fails because of a transient infrastructure problem. At Stage 1, someone eventually discovers the missing output and manually restarts the job. At Stage 2, CloudWatch detects the failure immediately and alerts the engineering team. At Stage 3, the system recognizes a known recoverable failure, retries the job automatically, verifies that the expected records were produced, and only escalates to a human if recovery fails.” So maturity changes the human role from: constantly operating the pipeline toward: handling exceptions the automation cannot safely resolve.  Connect to the earlier topics “This maturity arc depends on everything we’ve been discussing. You can’t safely automate remediation unless you have: Data contracts — to define acceptable behavior. Testing — to prevent bad changes from reaching production. Observability — to detect abnormal behavior. Lineage — to understand dependencies and impact. Governance — to constrain what automated systems are allowed to do. So self-healing isn’t a feature we simply turn on. It’s something we earn by building the underlying engineering controls.”  Important caution “I would not interpret Stage 3 as ‘remove humans from the system.’ Some remediation is safe to automate: retry, restart, scale, quarantine. Other situations require human judgment—especially when there is uncertainty about data correctness, privacy, security, or business impact. The objective is bounded automation, not unrestricted autonomy.”  Closing takeaway “The maturity progression is: Stage 1: BUILD — It works.Can we reliably produce value? Stage 2: OBSERVE — We can see it.Can we detect and understand problems? Stage 3: AUTOMATE — It can recover.Can we safely respond to known failure modes automatically? The important lesson is that production AI maturity isn’t just about having a more sophisticated model. It’s about making the entire data system increasingly observable, resilient, reproducible, and safely automated.”   Stage 1 — Functional (Lab 2 level):Data moves from sources to features. Pipeline runs. Quality is checked. Features are versioned.  Stage 2 — Observable (Lab 6 level):Pipeline health is visible. Failures alert. Drift is detected. Data quality SLAs are monitored.  Stage 3 — Self-Healing (advanced):Pipeline failures trigger automated remediation. Drift triggers automatic retraining. Data contracts enforce schema evolution automatically. Few teams reach this level.  NorthStar Labs map: Lab 2: functional pipeline → Stage 1 Lab 6: monitoring added → Stage 2 Stage 3 is the team project north star for ambitious teams  Notes: "Most enterprise teams are at Stage 1 or transitioning to Stage 2. Stage 3 is the aspiration. Your labs take NorthStar from Stage 1 (Lab 2) to Stage 2 (Lab 6). If your team project attempts Stage 3 behaviors — automated retraining triggers, self-healing data pipelines — that's exceptional work and will be recognized."
