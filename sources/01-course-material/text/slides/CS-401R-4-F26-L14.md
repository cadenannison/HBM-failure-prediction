## Slide 2

> Notes: QUESTION: If our churn model passed every offline evaluation gate, why can’t we ship it like a web app?  With a traditional web app, you ship new code, the same logic runs, users see the update, and if it breaks, rollback is nearly instant. AI systems break that mental model in five ways.  1. The model artifact and the serving code are separate things that must stay in sync. A version mismatch often fails silently rather than loudly.  2. Performance is probabilistic. A model can pass offline evaluation and still underperform on the live production distribution. You don’t truly know until production traffic hits it.  3. Rollback isn’t free. Re-serving a large artifact and warming a cold endpoint takes time.  4. Business outcomes are delayed. For churn, we won’t know whether the intervention worked for 4 to 8 weeks.  5. Components are coupled. Shipping a new churn model and a new feature pipeline together creates compound risk.  Now the matrix. The compound-risk quadrant, top right, is where production incidents live. Teams that combine a model update with a feature-store schema change can’t tell, when something fails, whether it was the model, the features, or their interaction. And the extreme case (feature change + model + RAG index + agent configuration all at once) is something you should never do. Deploy one thing at a time. > Notes: QUESTION: With six patterns available, how do we decide which one fits a given change?  Walk the table from top to bottom.  Big Bang, replace everything at once, is high risk and low complexity. It is never appropriate for production AI.  Blue/Green keeps two full environments and flips traffic atomically; we use it for major architecture changes. “Flips traffic atomically” “Atomic” comes from computing and means all at once or not at all, with no in-between state. The word comes from the Greek atomos, “indivisible”: the operation can’t be split into smaller pieces that happen separately. Atomicity gives Blue/Green its two main benefits: No in-between state to debug. Every request was served entirely by v2.3 or entirely by v3.0. Instant rollback. Rolling back is the same atomic flip in reverse, and Blue is still running and warm.   Canary sends a small slice of traffic to the new version and expands as metrics stay healthy; it is our default for routine model updates.  Shadow sends a copy of live traffic to the new model but never returns its answers to users. It is the lowest-risk option and the way to validate before any exposure.  Feature Flags let us choose which users see new behavior, independent of deployment.  A/B testing compares variants on business metrics over a longer window.  The three recommendations at the bottom are the rules of thumb for enterprise AI: default to canary, shadow first for major changes, and never Big Bang in production, even under deadline pressure. > Notes: QUESTION: If users never see a shadow model’s answers, what can it actually tell us about the new model?  Shadow deployment runs the new model next to production on live traffic, but its outputs are never shown to anyone. Walk the diagram: a live request goes to production v2.3, which answers the user. A copy of the same request, with the same features, goes to shadow v3.0, usually asynchronously so it can’t slow the user’s response. The shadow’s prediction is logged next to production’s, keyed by request ID. That’s why shadow is the lowest-risk pattern: real production data, zero user exposure.  On AWS, SageMaker supports this directly with a shadow variant on the endpoint; you choose what share of traffic to mirror. Typical practice: mirror 10 to 50 percent, run at least 24 hours (that’s on our readiness checklist), and log enough to reproduce every comparison.  So does it measure performance? Yes, at three levels, on different clocks.  1. Operational performance, immediately: latency, error rate, memory, throughput under real load. The v3.0 incident we’ll study later, a missing deprecated feature, would have shown up on the first shadow request with no customer affected.  2. Prediction behavior, within hours: how often the two models agree at the churn cutoff, the PSI between their score distributions, and which segments they disagree on most. This tells you how different the new model is, not whether it’s better.  3. Accuracy, once labels arrive: join the logged shadow predictions to real outcomes and compute AUC and precision at 70% recall for both models on identical live data. For churn, that means waiting 4 to 8 weeks.  What it cannot measure (bottom strip):  • Business impact. The shadow never triggers an action, so no offer is sent and there’s no retention lift to measure. That causal question needs an A/B test.  • Clean labels. Customers v2.3 flagged got offers, and some stayed because of them, which can make v3.0 look wrong. A small random holdout with no intervention reduces this.   The fix: a random holdout. Pick a small random slice of customers, say 5%, who get no retention offers, regardless of what any model says. Their outcomes are untouched, so their labels show true churn behavior. You compute AUC and precision at 70% recall for both models on that holdout, where the comparison is fair. The cost is the revenue from churners in that slice you deliberately don’t try to save, which is why it’s kept small.  • Side effects. A shadow agent can’t really send emails or issue refunds, or you’d do everything twice. Tool calls are stubbed and its planned actions are reviewed instead.  Bottom line: shadow validates; canary and A/B deploy and measure.

> Notes: QUESTION: Why does NorthStar advance its churn canary once a day instead of every fifteen minutes like a typical web app?  Mechanics first.  Step 1: deploy the new model to a small slice of traffic, the canary group.  Step 2: monitor that group for error rate, latency, prediction quality, and any business metrics you can measure.  Step 3: if the canary stays healthy across the observation window, expand to the next slice.  Step 4: if it shows anomalies, roll back automatically and immediately.  What counts as an “error” here An error is any request the canary variant couldn’t answer successfully: Server errors (5XX): the model container crashed or threw an exception. The v3.0 incident in the deck is the classic case: the model expected a deprecated feature, production didn’t send it, and inference failed. Client errors (4XX): the endpoint rejected the request. This usually means a bad payload, but a rise on the canary alone can mean v3.0 expects a different input format than callers are sending. Timeouts: the model took too long and the caller gave up.  The NorthStar schedule: Day 0 at 90/10, Day 1 at 70/30, Day 2 at 50/50, Day 3 at 100 percent, at which point v3.0 becomes production and v2.3 is archived. Each step observes for 24 hours.  Why 24 hours? For a web app you might advance every 15 minutes. For a churn model, the interesting prediction patterns don’t show up in 15 minutes. They emerge over a full day of diverse customer requests: weekday versus evening traffic, different segments, batch jobs. A 24-hour window gives statistical confidence before you increase exposure.  The health criteria on the right are checked every 15 minutes: canary error rate within 10 percent of production, P99 latency within 20 percent, and a population stability index on predictions under 0.15. That last one is the AI-specific check: it catches a model that is up and fast but predicting differently.  TRANSITION: So how do we actually tell SageMaker to split traffic 90/10? > Notes: QUESTION: How does a single SageMaker endpoint serve two different models and split traffic between them?  SageMaker endpoints support multiple production variants behind one endpoint. Each variant names a model, an instance type, an instance count, and a weight.  Walk the code. The Production variant serves v2.3 with weight 9. The Canary variant serves v3.0 with weight 1. SageMaker routes each request in proportion to the weights. The comments show the math: production’s share is its weight divided by the total, 9 of 10, so 90 percent; the canary gets 1 of 10, so 10 percent.  The key point is that the number you type is a relative weight, not a percentage. 9 and 1 happen to add to 10, so they look like percentages divided by ten, but 90 and 10 would give exactly the same split. The trap shows up when you change one weight without the others. Say you want the canary at 30 percent and you change its weight from 10 to 30 while production stays at 90: the total is now 120, so the canary actually gets 25 percent. Or add a second canary with weight 1 next to 9 and 1: production drops to about 82 percent. Raise one weight, and every other share shrinks. That trips people up in the lab.  Two design choices worth calling out. First, both variants use the same instance type. If the canary ran on bigger hardware, its latency would look better for reasons that have nothing to do with the model. Second, you don’t redeploy to advance the canary. You call UpdateEndpointWeightsAndCapacities to shift the weights to 7/3, then 5/5, then retire the old variant. No new endpoint, no cold start.  Note the canary has only one instance. It only needs to handle 10 percent of traffic; you scale it as you advance.  TRANSITION: Canary trades speed for safety: it takes days. > Notes: QUESTION: When is it worth paying for two complete production environments at the same time?  In blue/green, two complete, identical deployment environments exist simultaneously. Blue is current production and serves all live traffic. Green is the new version: deployed, smoke-tested, warm, and receiving zero traffic.  When you’re ready, the traffic router (API Gateway or an Application Load Balancer in front of the endpoints) switches 100 percent of traffic from Blue to Green in one atomic step. Blue stays running. If anything goes wrong, you flip back: no new deployment, no cold start, no delay. Compare that to canary: canary unfolds over days; blue/green switches in seconds.  The cost is running two full endpoints during the transition. For a couple of ml.m5.large instances over a two-hour release window, that’s a trivial cost for a major release. Running it permanently, though, doubles your serving bill, so blue/green is for planned releases, not always-on operation.  Rule of thumb: canary for routine model updates; blue/green for major architecture changes where you want instant rollback if something breaks. And for major changes, run shadow first, before Green ever receives a request.  TRANSITION: What does the switch itself look like in code? It’s smaller than you might expect.

> Notes: QUESTION: What are the three things a blue/green switch function must do, and in what order?  This Lambda is the entire switch. Three steps, in a deliberate order.  Step 1: verify that Green is healthy before switching. If the health check fails, raise an exception and stop. Never route production traffic to an endpoint that hasn’t passed its smoke tests.  Step 2: update the routing rule so the API Gateway or load balancer points at Green. Notice that nothing about either endpoint changes; only the router does. That is why the switch is atomic and why rollback is instant.  Step 3: log the deployment event: old version, new version, timestamp. That log becomes part of your Stage 7 deployment record and your audit trail.  A small correctness note for the lab: you’ll see older examples that use from= as a keyword argument. In Python, from is a reserved word, so that is a syntax error. Use names like from_endpoint and to_endpoint.  Rollback is just this same function with the arguments reversed. Have that call scripted and tested before you need it.   TRANSITION: Canary and blue/green both decide which version serves a request. Feature flags let us decide which users get the new behavior.

> Notes: QUESTION: How can we put new code into production without every user seeing the new behavior?  Feature flags decouple deployment from release. The code for both paths is in production, but a flag decides which customers get the new behavior.  NorthStar’s use case: Offer Generation v2, with improved RAG, is ready. Rather than turning it on for everyone, we roll it out to premium customers first.  Follow the diagram. A request arrives, and the service passes AppConfig some context about it: the customer’s ID and segment, looked up from the customer record. Rules never trigger on their own; they are evaluated against the context the application supplies. AppConfig then answers one question: is this customer in the rollout group? Premium customers: yes, all of them get v2. High-Value customers: 25 percent get v2 and the other 75 percent stay on v1. Everyone else stays on the legacy rule-based offers.  The code on the right is the flag in AppConfig’s multi-variant rule syntax. Variants are checked top to bottom, and the first match wins.  • offer_v2_premium matches when the segment equals "Premium" and returns true.  • offer_v2_hv_25 needs both conditions: the segment is "High-Value" AND the customer falls in the 25 percent split. split hashes the customerId into a fixed bucket, so it is sticky: the same customer gets the same answer on every request. That keeps the experience consistent and gives clean groups to measure.  • Everyone who matched neither rule, including the other 75 percent of High-Value customers, falls through to the default, false, which means v1 offers. Nobody is dropped; every request gets v1 or v2.  Note that 25 percent means 25 percent of High-Value customers, not of all traffic. If Premium is 10 percent of requests and High-Value is 20 percent, v2 serves 10 + 0.25 × 20 = 15 percent of total traffic.  One trap: stacked split rules interact. A later 25 percent split only sees the customers that earlier rules didn’t claim, so it can end up covering far fewer people than its number suggests.  The important property: changing these rules is a configuration change, not a deployment. You can widen the rollout, or hit the kill switch, in seconds.  AppConfig is the managed AWS service for this: it integrates with Lambda, supports gradual rollouts and targeting rules, and gives you an emergency kill switch. > Notes: QUESTION: What does a feature flag check look like in service code, and what is the hidden cost of leaving flags in place?  Inside the offer generation service, the flag check is a single branch. First, build the context: the customer’s ID and segment. These are exactly the values the AppConfig rules on the previous slide test: $segment for the eq checks, $customerId for the split. Then ask AppConfig for use_rag_offer_v2 with that context. Every flag that stays enabled long-term becomes a source of confusion: is this behavior for all users, or just flagged users? Flags multiply your test matrix. Establish a discipline: flags are temporary scaffolding for rollouts, not permanent configuration. Once the rollout completes, or is abandoned, remove the flag and clean up the dead code path. > Notes: QUESTION: Who decides to roll back a failing canary at 2 a.m., and why shouldn’t a person decide?  Manual canary monitoring means someone has to watch dashboards for three days. That doesn’t scale, and it doesn’t work at 2 a.m. Automated health gates make the decision for you.  The flow: a scheduled Lambda runs every 15 minutes during the canary window. It pulls canary and production metrics from CloudWatch, compares them, and classifies the result into one of three tiers.  Hard failure: error rate above 5 percent, or more than ten times production, or P99 latency more than twice production. The system rolls back immediately and pages on-call.  Soft warning: error rate between 1.5 and 5 percent, or latency between 1.5 and 2 times production. The system pauses advancement and alerts on-call but doesn’t roll back. A human makes the call in business hours.  Healthy: everything within bounds. Advance to the next traffic slice.  The immediate rollback on hard failure is non-negotiable. Don’t make the on-call engineer look at a dashboard and decide whether to roll back at 2 a.m. The system rolls back automatically, and the engineer wakes up to a notification like: “Canary rolled back at 02:14 — error rate 8.3% vs. production 0.4%.” They investigate in the morning. Note: AWS Systems Manager Parameter Store is a managed key-value store for configuration data, built into AWS Systems Manager. It gives applications one central, secure place to read settings from, instead of hard-coding them or scattering them across config files. What you store Plain settings (String): thresholds, endpoint names, feature switches, prompt text. Lists (StringList): comma-separated values. Secrets (SecureString): passwords, API keys and tokens, encrypted with AWS KMS. Key features Hierarchical names. Parameters are named like file paths, such as /northstar/offer-generation/prompt/v3.2. You can read a whole branch at once, and IAM can restrict access by path (the CodeBuild role can read /northstar/build/* but not /northstar/prod/*). Automatic versioning. Every update creates a new version, and you can read a specific one (name:3) or attach labels like prod. That gives you an audit trail of who changed what, and when. Integrations. Lambda, ECS, CodeBuild, CloudFormation and SDK calls can all read parameters directly. Cost. The standard tier is free. An advanced tier allows larger values and features like expiration policies, for a small charge. Why the L14 slides use it for prompts It lets you treat a prompt like configuration instead of code: The application reads the prompt at request time, so changing it doesn't require a redeploy. The active-version parameter is a pointer. Changing it from v3.2 to v3.1 is a one-call rollback. The same pointer pattern drives the RAG blue/green swap (/northstar/rag/active-kb-id). Caveats worth telling students Size limits. Standard-tier values max out at 4 KB, and advanced at 8 KB. That fits a modest prompt (the ~800-token offer prompt is roughly 3 KB), but long system prompts with examples won't fit. The common workaround is to store the prompt text in S3 and keep only the pointer in Parameter Store. Request-rate limits. The default throughput is modest. Reading from Parameter Store on every request at production volume can get throttled, so cache the value for 30–60 seconds. Rollback is still near-instant. Alternatives. For secrets that need automatic rotation, AWS Secrets Manager is the better fit. For feature flags with targeting rules and gradual rollouts, use AppConfig, as on the feature-flag slides. For prompts specifically, Amazon Bedrock Prompt Management offers versioned prompts built for that purpose. Parameter Store is the simplest general-purpose option of the group, which is why it works well for teaching the pointer pattern.

> Notes: Three testing failures that historically cost Lab 4 students the most points.  First: calling AWS from unit tests. The import itself is harmless; the problem is unit tests that actually call AWS. They slow down, require credentials, and break in any offline environment. In unit/, mock AWS calls — moto, or unittest.mock — and save real AWS calls for integration/.  Second: tests that pass on your laptop and fail in CodeBuild. Nine times out of ten, a fixture points to a local path — /Users/yourname/northstar/data/fixture.json. CodeBuild checks out your repo somewhere else. > Notes: This is the script you write for Lab 4: training/evaluate.py.  It parses three arguments — model directory, test-data directory, output directory — loads the model and test arrays, computes AUC, and writes evaluation.json, which is what the ConditionStep reads.  Two things to get right.  First, types. roc_auc_score returns a numpy float, and comparing a numpy float to 0.72 gives you a numpy.bool_. json.dump can't serialize numpy.bool_ — the job crashes with "Object of type bool_ is not JSON serializable," and the pipeline fails for a reason that has nothing to do with your model.
