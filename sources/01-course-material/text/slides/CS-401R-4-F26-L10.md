## Slide 6
- What Is XOps and Why It Matters
> Notes: KEY POINTS (say these; the detail below is reference) •  Building an AI system and operating one are different problems. •  Four disciplines that stack: DataOps, MLOps, LLMOps, AgentOps. •  Adoption outruns operational maturity. The gaps on the chart are percentage points; treat the figures as indicative. •  “We have a model in production” says almost nothing about maturity.  Presentation Notes Opening:This slide introduces the umbrella concept for this part of the course: XOps. The basic idea is straightforward. Building an AI system and operating an AI system are two different challenges. XOps is the family of operational disciplines that makes AI systems reliable, repeatable, observable, governable, and scalable in production.  XOps is a family, not a single discipline On the upper left, we have four major components of the XOps stack. DataOps focuses on the data pipeline. It asks: Is the data arriving? Is it correct? Is it fresh? Can we trace where it came from? Can we reproduce the transformations? MLOps focuses on the model lifecycle: training, experiment tracking, evaluation, registration, deployment, monitoring, and retraining. LLMOps extends these ideas to foundation-model applications. Now we have additional operational concerns such as prompts, retrieval pipelines, model evaluation, safety guardrails, and rapidly changing foundation models. And AgentOps addresses autonomous or semi-autonomous agents. Here we have to monitor not only model outputs but also actions, tool calls, permissions, state, and authority boundaries. The key idea is that each discipline corresponds to a different layer of the AI system.  Why the distinctions matter Imagine NorthStar eventually has all four. The customer and transaction pipelines require DataOps. Our churn model requires MLOps. A RAG-based customer assistant requires LLMOps. And an agent that can take actions on behalf of a customer or employee introduces AgentOps. These aren't competing approaches. They stack together. As the AI system becomes more capable, the operational surface area grows with it.  What happens without DataOps? The upper-right panel shows why these disciplines matter economically. Without DataOps, pipeline failures may go unnoticed for days. Worse, a pipeline may not technically fail at all—it may simply produce incomplete or corrupted data. That bad data can then propagate into model training. This is an important theme throughout XOps:  A system can be technically running while being operationally wrong. That's why we need quality gates, lineage, monitoring, and alerting—not just jobs that complete successfully. What happens without MLOps? The same problem appears at the model layer. Without MLOps, deployments become manual and slow. Rollback is difficult. Monitoring is inconsistent. And model quality can deteriorate silently after deployment. The organization may have excellent data scientists and excellent models, but if it takes weeks to safely move a model into production—or nobody knows when it stops performing—the business isn't capturing the potential value of the model. So XOps is ultimately about turning AI capability into sustainable production capability.  The maturity gap The lower portion of the slide illustrates an important industry pattern: organizations tend to be much further along in deploying technology than in operationalizing it. Look at the shape of the three comparisons. Many organizations have data pipelines, but fewer have mature DataOps practices. Many have ML models running in production, but fewer have automated the model lifecycle. And organizations are rapidly deploying LLM applications, while mature LLMOps practices lag behind. The exact percentages will vary by survey and by how maturity is defined, but the structural pattern is what matters: adoption tends to move faster than operational maturity.  Deployment is not operational maturity This distinction is critical. Saying “we have a model in production” tells us almost nothing about MLOps maturity. I want to know: Can you reproduce it? Can you identify the data and code that produced it? Was it evaluated against a baseline? Can you deploy a new version automatically? Can you roll it back? Are you monitoring drift? Can you retrain safely? Those questions tell us whether the model is operationalized, rather than merely deployed.  Why the maturity gap becomes expensive The gap between deployment and operational maturity creates technical debt. Initially, manual processes can seem cheaper. Why build CI/CD if an engineer can deploy the model? Why build monitoring if someone can check the dashboard? Why automate retraining if we only retrain occasionally? The problem appears when we scale from one model to ten models, from one data pipeline to dozens, or from one LLM application to an enterprise portfolio. Manual operations scale approximately with the number of systems. Automation, standards, and shared platforms allow us to scale much more efficiently. That's where the business case for XOps comes from.  Connect this to NorthStar NorthStar is intentionally following the same progression. We didn't begin with a complete XOps platform. Lab 1 established the infrastructure foundation. Lab 2 introduced DataOps through pipelines, Feature Store, quality controls, and lineage. Lab 3 introduced MLOps through experiment tracking, evaluation, and the Model Registry. The upcoming labs increasingly automate deployment, monitoring, rollback, retraining, and eventually cost and business-value governance. So the labs aren't isolated assignments. They progressively build the operational capabilities represented on this slide.  Key takeaway The central message is: Building AI proves that a model or application can work. XOps makes it possible to keep that AI system working reliably in production. And as our architecture evolves from data pipelines to ML models to foundation models and eventually agents, the corresponding operational disciplines evolve with it: DataOps → MLOps → LLMOps → AgentOps.  Transition:We'll start by examining DataOps and MLOps in more depth—because they establish the operational patterns we'll reuse later. Then, when we move into LLMOps and AgentOps, we'll see that many of the same principles remain: automation, testing, observability, governance, and controlled promotion into production.

As we move from left to right, we are progressively reducing manual work and increasing automation, governance, reliability, and ultimately business value. Lab 2 — DataOps:Lab 2 adds the data foundation: automated pipelines, data-quality gates, feature management, and lineage. Each capability becomes another layer of the operating platform. By Lab 7:The NorthStar platform has reached MLOps Level 3: a largely automated lifecycle incorporating monitoring, deployment safeguards, rollback, and operational governance. When we add DataOps and FinOps capabilities around that lifecycle, we get the broader XOps stack. Closing/transition:The important idea is that XOps maturity is not a product we install—it is a set of capabilities we progressively build. The seven labs are intentionally structured to take us through that journey, one operational capability at a time.

## Slide 10
- XOps: AWS Services Stack
> Notes: KEY POINTS (say these; the detail below is reference) •  Each discipline operates a different artifact: data, model, foundation-model application, agent actions. •  The DataOps column is a reference stack. NorthStar uses Glue Workflows and CloudWatch in place of MWAA, dbt and Glue Data Quality. •  CI/CD, identity, observability, governance and cost controls are shared across all four.  Presentation Notes Opening This slide gives us the big-picture view of the XOps stack. The important idea is that DataOps, MLOps, LLMOps, and AgentOps are not four competing architectures. They address different operational problems at different layers of an AI system. As we move from left to right, the artifact we're operating becomes progressively more complex. DataOps — trusted data Start with DataOps. The primary artifact here is data. Our objective is to make that data trustworthy, reproducible, discoverable, and usable downstream. That requires orchestration, transformation, quality checks, cataloging and lineage, and event-driven execution. In our AWS example, that might involve MWAA, Glue and dbt, Glue Data Quality, SageMaker Catalog, and EventBridge. The output isn't merely data. It is: Validated, discoverable data that downstream systems can trust. Everything else depends on that foundation. MLOps — reliable models MLOps builds on that data foundation, but now our operational artifact is the machine-learning model and its lifecycle. We need repeatable training pipelines, experiment tracking, consistent features, evaluation gates, a Model Registry, deployment, and production monitoring. For NorthStar, this is where the pieces from Labs 2, 3, and 4 start coming together. Our Feature Store gives us consistent features. SageMaker Pipelines automates training and evaluation. MLflow or SageMaker Experiments provides experiment tracking. The Model Registry governs candidate models. Model Monitor and our serving infrastructure help us operate those models after deployment. So the MLOps outcome is: Governed model artifacts and reliable production endpoints. LLMOps — foundation-model applications Now we move into LLMOps. The artifact changes again. In many foundation-model applications, we don't own or retrain the underlying model weights. Instead, we operate the system surrounding the foundation model. That means versioning prompts and configurations, continuously evaluating responses, enforcing guardrails, monitoring application behavior, tracking token usage and cost, and managing model access. For an AWS implementation, Amazon Bedrock becomes an important serving layer, with Bedrock evaluation and Guardrails supporting the operational lifecycle. The output of LLMOps is therefore not simply an LLM. It is a: Governed foundation-model application—with controlled prompts, evaluation, access, safety, observability, and cost. AgentOps — safe autonomous execution AgentOps introduces another level of complexity. An agent doesn't merely generate text. It can reason, call tools, access memory, invoke APIs, and take actions. That means our operational concern shifts from monitoring outputs to monitoring execution. We need to know: What did the agent do? Which tools did it call? What happened inside the workflow? How long did each step take? Did it exceed its authority? When should a human approve an action? And what happens when something fails? This is why tracing becomes so important in AgentOps. In the current AWS architecture, AgentCore provides capabilities specifically intended to operate and observe agents, including telemetry that integrates with CloudWatch and OpenTelemetry. The desired outcome is: Governed agent actions and observable execution. Notice the progression Look across the four columns. The progression is roughly: DataOps: Can I trust the data? MLOps: Can I trust the model? LLMOps: Can I trust the foundation-model application? AgentOps: Can I trust the actions the autonomous system takes? That's a useful mental model for distinguishing these disciplines. But don't interpret this as four silos The arrows say “builds on” rather than implying a literal data pipeline. That's deliberate. Not every LLM system needs a custom ML model, and not every agent requires every capability shown in the preceding columns. Instead, the point is that the operational disciplines accumulate. AgentOps doesn't eliminate LLMOps. LLMOps doesn't eliminate MLOps. And none of them eliminate the need for reliable data. The shared XOps foundation Finally, notice the bar across the bottom. All four disciplines share some common operational foundations: CI/CD, identity and access, observability, governance, and cost controls. This is one reason I prefer thinking about these disciplines collectively as XOps. We're repeatedly applying many of the same operational principles—automation, testing, observability, governance, reproducibility, and ownership—to different AI artifacts. Key takeaway The takeaway is: XOps is not one tool or one pipeline. It is a family of operational disciplines applied to increasingly complex AI artifacts. A simple way to remember the stack is: DataOps → trust the dataMLOps → trust the model lifecycleLLMOps → trust the foundation-model applicationAgentOps → trust the autonomous execution And as we move to the right, observability and governance become more important—not less—because the system gains greater ability to make decisions and take actions.

## Slide 11
- XOps Adoption Sequence and Common Failures
> Notes: KEY POINTS (say these; the detail below is reference) •  Build in dependency order: data, then models, then LLM applications, then agents. •  The stages overlap. This is a sequence of dependencies, not four separate projects. •  Three failure patterns: tooling without process, process without automation, XOps as a separate team.  Presentation Notes — XOps Adoption Sequence and Common Failures Opening:This slide answers two practical questions: In what order should we build XOps capabilities, and what tends to go wrong along the way? The key message is that DataOps, MLOps, LLMOps, and AgentOps are related disciplines, but they don't exist independently. Each builds on capabilities established earlier. As we move from data pipelines to models to LLM applications to autonomous agents, both system complexity and operational risk increase. The sequence shown here is a recommended progression—not a rule that says an organization must completely finish one discipline before starting the next.  1. Start with DataOps The foundation is DataOps. Before we worry about sophisticated models or agents, we need trustworthy data. That means automated data pipelines, quality and observability, data contracts, lineage, and documented datasets. The outcome we're trying to create is simple: Reliable, trustworthy data that everything else can build upon. This is why we spent significant time on DataOps in Lab 2. If the underlying data is unreliable, everything downstream inherits that problem. A highly automated ML pipeline trained on bad data simply allows us to produce a bad model faster.  2. Build MLOps on top of the data foundation Once we have reliable data and stable features, we can mature MLOps. Now we're concerned with repeatable model training and deployment. That includes Feature Store and stable data interfaces, automated training pipelines, evaluation, Model Registry, and CI/CD for models. The desired outcome is: Reliable models delivered consistently to production. This is the progression we're making from Lab 3 into Lab 4. Lab 3 introduced experiment tracking, evaluation, and registration. Lab 4 begins turning those individual steps into an automated pipeline. The dependency is important: MLOps does not eliminate the need for DataOps—it depends on it.  3. LLMOps introduces a different operational problem The third stage is LLMOps. Here we're no longer managing only traditional predictive models. We're operating applications built around foundation models. That creates new operational artifacts and risks. Prompts need version control. Evaluations need to become systematic. We need routing and gateway controls. And because foundation models are consumed by tokens and API calls, cost becomes an operational metric alongside quality and latency. The outcome is controlled LLM usage with: quality, safety, observability, and cost visibility. Notice that LLMOps doesn't replace MLOps. It extends the operational stack for a different kind of AI system.  4. AgentOps adds action and authority Finally, we reach AgentOps. This is where operational discipline becomes particularly important because an agent can potentially do more than generate an answer—it can take actions in other systems. So we need full execution traces, reliability patterns such as limits and circuit breakers, human-in-the-loop gates for sensitive actions, and strong observability and governance.  The desired outcome is: Safe, observable agents that take actions within explicitly defined guardrails. This is also why AgentOps appears last in the sequence. The progression is roughly: Data creates information → models make predictions → LLMs generate and reason → agents act. As the system gains more authority, the operational controls need to become stronger. These stages overlap One caution when interpreting the arrows across the top: don't read them as four completely separate projects. You don't have to “finish DataOps” before writing your first ML model, and you don't have to reach perfect MLOps maturity before experimenting with an LLM. The sequence tells us where the dependencies lie. If we're trying to put an autonomous agent into production while we still have unreliable data, no evaluation framework, weak observability, and no governance controls, we've skipped important foundations. So think of this as progressive capability accumulation, not four isolated phases. Three common failure patterns The bottom half shows what happens when organizations try to shortcut this progression.  Anti-pattern 1: Tooling without process The first failure is buying tools before defining the operating process. An organization might purchase an MLOps platform, an LLM gateway, monitoring products, or an orchestration system and assume the tools themselves create operational maturity. They don't. If teams haven't agreed on definitions, ownership, quality gates, deployment policies, and operating standards, the tools simply automate inconsistency. The typical result is: Expensive tooling, low trust, and low adoption. The principle here is: Define the process first; use tooling to enforce and automate it.  Anti-pattern 2: Process without automation The opposite failure is also common. An organization may define excellent processes—but execute all of them manually. People fill out checklists. Someone manually runs tests. Someone copies artifacts between environments. Someone checks dashboards and sends an email when something looks wrong. That can work with one model. It becomes a bottleneck with 10, 50, or 100 models. Manual processes are slower, harder to reproduce, and more susceptible to human error. Eventually the people operating the process become the constraint. The typical result is: High effort, fragile operations, and a system that doesn't scale. So maturity requires both: Process defines what should happen; automation makes sure it happens consistently.  Anti-pattern 3: XOps as a separate team The third failure is organizational. Suppose we create a centralized “XOps team” and tell every product team: “When you're ready for production, throw your model over the wall to these people.” We've recreated the same problem DevOps was intended to solve. The central team becomes a queue. They lack the context of the product teams. Decisions slow down. Shadow processes begin appearing because teams work around the bottleneck. The typical result is: Low adoption and a centralized team that gets bypassed. A stronger model is for a platform or XOps function to provide shared capabilities, standards, templates, guardrails, and self-service tooling, while product teams retain responsibility for the systems they operate. In other words: XOps should be an enabler, not a handoff. How the two halves connect The top and bottom of this slide really tell one story. The top says: Build capabilities progressively. The bottom says: Don't confuse capability with buying tools, writing procedures, or creating another silo. Successful XOps requires all three pieces to work together: Process + Automation + Operating Model. Tools without process create chaos. Process without automation doesn't scale. And automation centralized in a disconnected team creates a bottleneck. Connection to NorthStar NorthStar follows this progression deliberately. We established the infrastructure foundation first. Then Lab 2 built DataOps capabilities. Lab 3 introduced MLOps capabilities around training, experiment tracking, evaluation, and registration. Lab 4 adds automated pipelines and CI/CD. From there, we can apply the same operational thinking to foundation-model applications and eventually agents. So the labs aren't simply teaching us a sequence of AWS services. They're progressively assembling the operational capabilities required to safely run increasingly sophisticated AI systems.  Key takeaway The sentence I'd leave students with is: Don't automate chaos, don't scale manual processes, and don't turn XOps into another silo. Build a trustworthy data foundation, automate the model lifecycle, extend those controls to LLMs, and add stronger governance as systems gain the ability to act autonomously. The goal isn't simply to reach AgentOps. The goal is to increase AI capability without allowing operational risk to increase faster than our ability to control it.

## Slide 13
- DataOps: Principles and Northstar Comparison
- Lab 4 adds automated CI/CD testing; multi-environment promotion is out of scope (dev only).
> Notes: KEY POINTS (say these; the detail below is reference) •  Six DataOps capabilities: orchestration, quality gates, observability, version control, testing, environment parity. •  NorthStar is solid on the first four; testing and environment parity are the gaps. •  Maturity is about properties of the system, not which tools it uses.  Presentation Notes Opening:This slide defines what we mean by DataOps maturity. DataOps is not simply building ETL pipelines or moving data into S3. It's the set of engineering practices that makes those pipelines automated, reliable, observable, reproducible, and safe to change. On the left are six core principles. On the right, we're comparing what NorthStar has achieved after Lab 2 with what we would expect from a fully mature production platform.  1. Pipeline orchestration The first principle is pipeline orchestration. In a mature DataOps environment, transformations don't depend on someone manually starting jobs in the correct order. The dependencies are encoded into the workflow, and execution is triggered by a schedule or an event. NorthStar has already made meaningful progress here with scheduled Glue jobs. The longer-term production standard goes further: pipelines should be parameterized, work consistently across environments, and ideally recover gracefully from predictable failures. The principle is simple: people shouldn't be the orchestration engine.  2. Data quality gates The second capability is data quality gates. It's not enough for a pipeline to complete successfully. We need to know whether the data it produced is actually acceptable. For NorthStar, we've introduced CloudWatch metrics and alerts. That's a good foundation. At full production maturity, we'd define comprehensive quality rules at important boundaries—schema checks, null thresholds, ranges, uniqueness, freshness, volume, and other business-specific expectations. And importantly, violations should be able to stop the pipeline before bad data propagates downstream. For AI systems, that matters enormously because bad data can produce a technically valid but operationally useless model.  3. Observability Third is observability. Monitoring tells us whether something failed. Observability helps us understand what is happening inside the system and why. NorthStar has CloudWatch dashboards that give us visibility into pipeline behavior. A mature environment expands this to end-to-end metrics, logs, traces, alerts, latency, volume trends, and data lineage. The operational question is: Can we quickly determine what ran, what changed, where a problem occurred, and what downstream systems were affected?  4. Version control The fourth principle is version control, and NorthStar is already in relatively good shape here. Our Glue job code is stored in Git. That means we can identify changes, review them, reproduce previous versions, and connect pipeline behavior back to specific code. But production maturity goes beyond versioning Python scripts. Ideally, we version code, configuration, infrastructure definitions, schemas, and relevant data or feature definitions, and connect those versions to CI/CD. The goal is reproducibility from a known state—not “this is approximately what we were running last month.”  5. Testing The fifth principle is one of our remaining gaps: testing. We've introduced unit testing, but integration testing is still partial. A mature DataOps platform needs multiple layers of tests. Unit tests verify individual transformation functions. Integration tests verify that pipeline components work together. And contract tests verify that data crossing a boundary satisfies the schema and expectations of the downstream consumer. Eventually, these tests should execute automatically as part of CI/CD. This changes testing from something an engineer remembers to do into an enforced condition for promotion.  6. Environment parity Our other major gap is environment parity. Ideally, development, staging, and production use the same pipeline logic and infrastructure patterns. The environment-specific differences should come from configuration—not from entirely different implementations. NorthStar currently emphasizes a development environment in the course. In a production platform, we'd expect equivalent dev, staging, and production environments with automated promotion between them. Why does that matter? Because “it worked in development” is only meaningful when development resembles production closely enough for the result to transfer. Reading the maturity table If you look across the table, NorthStar is already strong in the first four areas: orchestration, quality gates, observability, and version control. The yellow indicators highlight where we still have work to do: comprehensive testing and environment parity. This is an important way to think about maturity. We don't simply say, “NorthStar has DataOps.” We ask: Which DataOps capabilities do we have, and how mature is each one? Connection to the later labs The message at the bottom is important: we don't have to solve every operational problem in Lab 2. Lab 2 establishes the DataOps foundation. Later labs build on that foundation. > Notes: KEY POINTS (say these; the detail below is reference) •  Orchestration answers: what runs, when, in what order, under what conditions. •  Read the DAG by dependencies: ingest in parallel, then ETL, then Feature Store, then training. •  For AI, an orchestration mistake produces a valid-looking model trained on incomplete data.  Presentation Notes Opening:This slide introduces a core DataOps problem: orchestration. A production data pipeline isn't simply a collection of Glue jobs. Those jobs have dependencies, and they have to execute in the right order. For NorthStar, we can't build features until the source data has arrived, and we shouldn't start model training until feature engineering and our data-quality checks have completed successfully. The question orchestration answers is: What runs when, in what order, and under what conditions?  The orchestration problem Without orchestration, an engineer might manually start one job, wait for it to finish, start the next job, check the results, and eventually launch training. That may work with two jobs. It doesn't scale when we have dozens of jobs, multiple dependencies, failures, retries, and different execution times. More importantly, manual sequencing creates operational risk. Run something too early and we can train a model on incomplete or stale data. So the dependencies need to become part of the platform rather than something an engineer remembers.  The NorthStar orchestration stack On the left are three AWS services with different responsibilities. AWS Glue Workflows handles the core data-pipeline orchestration. It lets us chain Glue jobs together and define dependencies between them. Amazon EventBridge gives us event-driven triggering. Instead of saying, “Run every day whether or not new data exists,” we can react to events such as new data arriving in S3. And AWS Step Functions becomes useful when orchestration grows more sophisticated—for example, when we need conditional logic, parallel branches, retries, or coordination across services. We'll see more of that kind of orchestration as we move toward CI/CD. The important point is that these services are complementary. The orchestration tool should match the complexity of the workflow.  Reading the DAG The center shows our NorthStar workflow as a DAG—a directed acyclic graph. “Directed” means execution moves in a defined direction through the dependencies. “Acyclic” means we don't create an endless loop back to an earlier job. Rather than reading this simply from top to bottom, look at the dependency relationships.  Steps 1 and 2 — ingest in parallel At the top, we have two ingestion jobs. Step 1 synchronizes the customer data, taking about 15 minutes. Step 2 synchronizes the transaction data, taking about 20 minutes. These jobs don't depend on each other, so they can execute in parallel. That's one of the benefits of orchestration: we don't serialize work unnecessarily.  Steps 3 and 4 — feature engineering Once the required ingestion work completes, we can begin the two ETL jobs. The customer feature extraction job takes roughly 30 minutes. The transaction aggregation job takes roughly 45 minutes. These create the engineered inputs that eventually feed our Feature Store. Again, the DAG tells the platform which upstream jobs each transformation depends on. We aren't relying on timing assumptions such as “the file is usually there by 2:30.” We are relying on explicit completion dependencies.  Step 5 — Feature Store ingestion Only after both feature-engineering branches have completed do we move to Step 5 and ingest the engineered features into the SageMaker Feature Store. This is a synchronization point. Even if the customer-feature branch finishes early, the workflow waits for the transaction branch. We don't want to publish a partially refreshed feature set and then allow downstream training to treat it as complete. This is a good example of why dependency management is also a data-quality mechanism.  Step 6 — handoff to model training Finally, once the Feature Store has been refreshed—and assuming the required data-quality gates pass—the workflow can trigger our model-training pipeline. Notice the boundary here. The Glue workflow is primarily orchestrating the DataOps side of NorthStar. The output then becomes an input or trigger for the MLOps side. Presentation Notes Opening:This slide shows five common DataOps anti-patterns. Notice that none of these are particularly sophisticated technical failures. They're shortcuts that often seem reasonable when we're moving quickly, but become expensive and risky once a data pipeline moves into production. The general principle is: production data pipelines should be reproducible, observable, configurable, and tested.  1. The Notebook Pipeline:The first anti-pattern is using a Jupyter notebook as the production data pipeline. Notebooks are excellent for exploration and development. The problem occurs when somebody has to open a notebook and manually run cells to produce production data. Now the pipeline depends on that person's knowledge, the state of their environment, and potentially even the order in which they execute the cells. A simple warning sign is: if production runs from an .ipynb file, we should ask why. For NorthStar, the fix is to move those transformations into parameterized AWS Glue jobs stored in version control. The notebook can help us develop the transformation, but it shouldn't become the production execution environment.  2. No Error Handling:The second anti-pattern is allowing a pipeline to fail silently. Imagine that one of our source files is missing or malformed. The ETL job processes everything else and writes a partial dataset. Technically, the job may appear to have produced output—but downstream systems now treat incomplete data as if it were correct. For machine learning, this is especially dangerous because bad data can produce a perfectly valid-looking model artifact. The fix is explicit error handling. Define what constitutes failure, stop the pipeline when those conditions occur, and use CloudWatch alerts so someone knows immediately. A useful principle is: partial success should not masquerade as complete success.  3. Hardcoded Paths:The third anti-pattern is hardcoding S3 bucket names, paths, account-specific values, or environment settings directly into ETL code. For example, if the Python script literally contains the production S3 path, moving that same pipeline from development to test to production requires changing the source code. That creates configuration drift and makes deployments harder to reproduce. Instead, we want the same code running in every environment, with environment-specific values supplied through parameters, environment variables, or infrastructure defined with Terraform. This connects directly to the idea of environment parity: dev, test, and production should differ in configuration—not in the underlying pipeline logic.  4. No Testing:The fourth anti-pattern is pipeline code with no automated tests. A transformation running successfully once does not demonstrate that it is correct. Data transformations are software, and they need to be tested like software. For example, if we calculate recency_days, normalize transaction amounts, or aggregate 90-day purchase frequency, we should have tests that provide known inputs and verify the expected outputs. For NorthStar, that means using something like pytest to test transformation functions before the Glue job is promoted to production. The phrase on the slide captures the issue well: “It worked once” is not a quality standard.  5. Manual Quality Checks:The fifth anti-pattern is relying on a person to run SQL queries every so often to check data quality. Reviews happen late, inconsistently, or not at all when that person is busy. The fix is an automated quality gate with defined thresholds. People review the alerts the gate raises, not the raw data. Connecting the anti-patterns:These problems correspond directly to DataOps maturity capabilities. The notebook pipeline is a problem of orchestration and version control. Silent failures are an observability and quality-gate problem. Hardcoded paths violate environment parity. Untested transformations are obviously a testing problem. And manual quality checks are a quality-gate problem. So DataOps isn't primarily about adding more tools. It's about systematically removing these fragile operating practices.  Key takeaway:The goal is to make the production pipeline boring and predictable. The code is versioned. Configuration is externalized. Tests run automatically. Failures are explicit. The key idea is the arrow across the bottom: as we move to the right, manual effort decreases while automation and business value increase.  Level 1 — Manual MLOps Most teams begin here. A data scientist works in a Jupyter notebook, runs scripts manually, examines the metrics, saves the model, and then somebody copies that model into production. There's nothing inherently wrong with this during experimentation. The problem is using this approach as the operating model for production. At this level, data preparation is manual, training is manual, evaluation is largely manual, deployment is manual, and monitoring may amount to hoping nothing breaks. The biggest issue is reproducibility. If the person who created the model leaves, can someone else reproduce exactly what they did? This is the technical debt we're trying to eliminate.  Level 2 — Automated Training Pipeline The second level is our immediate NorthStar target in Lab 4. Now we take the activities that were performed manually and encode them into a repeatable pipeline: Data → preparation → training → evaluation → Model Registry. For NorthStar, this is where SageMaker Pipelines becomes important. Instead of an engineer remembering the correct sequence of steps, the pipeline knows the sequence. We also introduce quality gates, so only models meeting our evaluation criteria can progress. But notice the limitation: deployment may still require a manual action. We've automated model creation, but not yet the entire path from a change to production. That's why this is an important maturity milestone, but not the end state.  Level 3 — Full CI/CD for ML Level 3 extends automation across the entire lifecycle and represents the industry-standard target for mature ML organizations. Now the process can begin with a code change, new data, or a schedule. The CI/CD system automatically triggers training and evaluation. The candidate goes through a quality gate, and models that satisfy our criteria can progress into deployment and validation. Monitoring then observes the production system. If model quality or data quality deteriorates, alerts fire and retraining can be triggered. So the lifecycle becomes: Trigger → Train → Evaluate → Gate → Deploy → Monitor → Retrain. This is where MLOps begins to look much more like a continuously operating production system rather than a sequence of data-science projects. The difference between Level 2 and Level 3 This distinction is worth emphasizing. At Level 2, we have automated the training workflow. At Level 3, we have automated the model lifecycle. That's a significant difference. A training pipeline answers: “Can I reproducibly produce and evaluate a model?” Full CI/CD answers: “Can a change safely travel from source code or new data all the way into production with automated controls along the way?” That's the maturity jump we're working toward across the NorthStar labs.  Level 4 — Self-Optimizing The fourth level is much more advanced and still relatively uncommon. Here we begin closing the feedback loop automatically. New data arrives. Production performance is continuously measured. The system can retrain or even continuously learn, evaluate competing variants through A/B testing, and feed production results back into the lifecycle. In other words, the system increasingly answers not just: “Should we deploy this model?” but: “How should we continuously improve the model based on what is happening in the real world?” This can be powerful, but it also introduces significant governance and operational risk. We shouldn't automate decisions simply because we technically can. That's why relatively few organizations operate at this level. Higher maturity isn't automatically better One caution: the goal isn't necessarily to get every system to Level 4. For many enterprise applications, Level 3 may be the appropriate target. If a model affects consequential business decisions, we may intentionally retain human approval or review gates. The appropriate level of automation depends on the risk, economics, frequency of change, and business requirements. So maturity doesn't mean “remove every human.” It means automate the parts that are predictable and repeatable while deliberately placing controls around the parts that require judgment.  Where NorthStar fits For NorthStar, we are moving deliberately through this progression. Earlier labs gave us the infrastructure, data pipelines, Feature Store, experiment tracking, and Model Registry. Lab 4 moves us toward Level 2 by automating the training workflow. Subsequent work adds CI/CD, safer deployment, monitoring, drift detection, retraining triggers, rollback, and cost governance—moving us progressively toward a mature Level 3 operating model. So each lab isn't simply adding another AWS service. Each lab removes another manual step from the model lifecycle and replaces it with a repeatable, governed capability. Prepare Features — ProcessingStep We start with feature preparation using a SageMaker ProcessingStep and an SKLearnProcessor. The pipeline reads the engineered features from our Feature Store, performs any final preparation required for modeling, and produces separate training and validation datasets. This is important for reproducibility. We don't want an engineer manually exporting a dataset or manipulating it in a notebook before every training run. The preparation logic is part of the pipeline itself. The outputs of this step become explicit inputs to the training step.  2. Train Churn Model — TrainingStep Next, the TrainingStep launches our XGBoost training job. Notice what we're doing here operationally: the training environment, inputs, algorithm, and outputs are all defined as part of the workflow. Evaluate Model — ProcessingStep Training successfully does not mean the model is good enough for production. So the third step runs our evaluation script—evaluate.py—using another ProcessingStep. Presentation Notes Opening:This slide highlights several common MLOps anti-patterns—practices that may work during experimentation but become dangerous when we move into production. The recurring theme is that production ML requires reproducibility, automated gates, meaningful evaluation, and operational controls.  1. Model in a Notebook:The first anti-pattern is treating the notebook as the production system. We train a model in Jupyter, save a .pkl file, copy it to S3, and manually configure an endpoint. That may work once, but immediately ask: Which code produced this model? Which data? Which hyperparameters? Which environment? And how do I roll back? If we cannot answer those questions reliably, we don't really have a production model—we have an artifact somebody happened to deploy. The fix is to move the lifecycle into SageMaker Pipelines, the Model Registry, and CI/CD. The notebook remains useful for experimentation, but production execution must be reproducible.  2. No gate in the pipeline:The second anti-pattern is more subtle. We may have automated training, but automation by itself isn't enough. If every model produced by the pipeline gets deployed, we've simply automated a bad decision process. This is why we introduced the ConditionStep. For NorthStar, we might require an AUC of at least 0.72. If the model doesn't meet the threshold, the pipeline stops. A useful principle here is: automate the decision criteria, not just the execution.  3. Missing baseline model:But an absolute threshold creates another problem. Imagine our new model achieves an AUC of 0.74. It passes the 0.72 gate—but what if the model currently in production has an AUC of 0.78? Technically, our candidate passed the gate. Operationally, we just deployed a worse model. That's why mature pipelines compare the candidate against a production baseline or champion model. We can require the new model not merely to be good, but to be measurably better—for example, by a defined improvement margin. So the question changes from “Is this model good?” to “Is this model better than what we already have?”  4. Ignoring class imbalance:The fourth anti-pattern demonstrates why choosing the right metric matters. Suppose 88% of our customers don't churn and only 12% do. A model could predict “no churn” for every customer and achieve 88% accuracy. That sounds impressive until we realize the model identified zero churners—the exact customers we built the model to find. For imbalanced classification problems, accuracy alone can therefore be misleading. We need metrics such as ROC-AUC, precision, recall, F1, and potentially precision-recall AUC, depending on the business objective. We may also need appropriate class weighting.  5. No deployment testing:The fifth anti-pattern is deploying to a production endpoint without checking that the endpoint actually serves predictions correctly. The model can be excellent and the endpoint can still be broken: wrong container, wrong input schema, missing preprocessing. The minimum fix is a smoke test. After deployment, send a test request and verify that the response matches the expected schema.  Connecting these anti-patterns:Notice that these aren't primarily algorithm problems. XGBoost could be working perfectly in every example. These are operational-system problems: No reproducibility creates deployment risk. No gate creates quality risk. No baseline creates regression risk. The wrong metric creates decision risk. No deployment testing creates serving risk. That's exactly why MLOps exists. We're engineering the system around the model, not just the model itself.  Key takeaway:A mature MLOps pipeline should make the safe path the automatic path. Models should be reproducibly built, evaluated against meaningful criteria, compared with the production baseline, registered and versioned, and only then allowed to progress toward deployment.  Transition:So when reviewing an ML pipeline, don't ask only, “Does it train a model?” Ask a more important question: “What prevents a bad model from reaching production?” If the answer is “an engineer will hopefully notice,” we still have MLOps work to do.

## Slide 31
> Notes: Presentation Notes — Use Case: Enterprise Technical Support System Opening / setup This graphic illustrates how the AI development spectrum can become a real production architecture. Imagine a company that manufactures or operates industrial equipment—motors, pumps, compressors, turbines, or manufacturing machinery. When equipment begins behaving abnormally, an engineer needs to determine three things quickly: What is happening? Why is it happening? And what should we do about it? The key point in this example is that we don’t necessarily choose between prompt engineering, RAG, fine-tuning, and traditional machine learning. A mature AI system may combine all four because each solves a different part of the problem. Stage 1 — Prompt Engineering: Start Simple We begin on the left with prompt engineering. Suppose an engineer reports: “The motor is overheating and shutting down. What could be the cause, and how do I fix it?” We can immediately give that question to a capable foundation model using a carefully constructed troubleshooting prompt. The prompt might instruct the model to behave as an industrial maintenance assistant, ask clarifying questions, identify likely causes, and provide a systematic diagnostic procedure. This is attractive because we can have a prototype running very quickly—potentially within hours. Notice that we haven’t trained anything. We’re leveraging capabilities already present in the foundation model. But we quickly encounter a limitation: the model knows general engineering concepts, but it doesn’t necessarily know our equipment, our maintenance history, or our company’s procedures. That takes us to Stage 2. Stage 2 — RAG: Add Company Knowledge Now we introduce Retrieval-Augmented Generation, or RAG.  The company may have thousands of equipment manuals, service bulletins, maintenance records, troubleshooting guides, and other internal documents. Rather than attempting to put all of this information into a prompt—or retraining the model every time a document changes—we create a searchable knowledge base. When the engineer submits a question, the system retrieves the most relevant information at runtime and supplies it to the foundation model as context. Now the model isn’t answering only from what it learned during its original training. It can answer using the organization’s current documentation. This gives us an important distinction: Prompt engineering tells the model how we want it to approach the problem. RAG gives the model the information it needs to solve the problem. And critically, RAG still doesn’t require us to modify the model’s weights. Stage 3 — Fine-Tuning: Change Model Behavior Eventually we may discover another problem. Perhaps the model has access to the correct information but doesn’t consistently follow the company’s diagnostic methodology. Maybe engineers must perform tests in a specific order, classify failures using an internal taxonomy, or produce reports according to a strict schema. This is where fine-tuning may become appropriate. We can collect historical examples of expert diagnoses, troubleshooting cases, step-by-step procedures, correct diagnoses, and examples of properly structured outputs. We then use those examples to adapt the foundation model. The distinction from RAG is worth emphasizing:  RAG primarily supplies knowledge. Fine-tuning primarily changes model behavior. After fine-tuning, we want the model to respond more consistently according to the organization’s specialized diagnostic process. But one important source of information is still missing from this architecture. The equipment itself. Stage 4 — Custom Predictive Models: Use the Sensor Data Modern industrial equipment may generate enormous amounts of sensor data: temperature, vibration, pressure, voltage, operating history, and previous failure information. This data is fundamentally different from manuals and troubleshooting documents. Suppose vibration has been slowly increasing for six weeks while motor temperature has simultaneously risen under a particular load condition. An LLM isn’t necessarily the best tool for discovering that predictive pattern. Instead, we might train a specialized machine-learning model on historical sensor data and equipment failures. That could be a traditional tabular model, a time-series model, or another specialized predictive architecture. Its job might be to estimate equipment health or identify the probability of an impending failure. This illustrates an important design principle:  Not every AI problem is an LLM problem. We choose the model architecture that fits the data and the prediction task. Putting Everything Together The bottom half of the graphic is the most important part. Notice that the production architecture contains multiple models working together. An engineer’s question enters the AI application. Prompt engineering interprets and orchestrates the request. RAG retrieves relevant company knowledge. A fine-tuned model produces a response following the organization’s diagnostic procedures. Meanwhile, a predictive model analyzes the equipment’s sensor data. The system can then combine those results. Instead of simply producing a generic chatbot response, the engineer might receive: a diagnosis and possible root cause, a step-by-step troubleshooting procedure, the relevant equipment manuals and service documents, an assessment of equipment health and potential failure risk, and recommended actions and next steps. That is a much more interesting system than simply putting a chat interface in front of an LLM.  Architecture insight — These Stages Are Cumulative There’s another subtle point in the graphic. When we move from Stage 1 to Stage 2, we don’t necessarily throw away prompt engineering. When we introduce fine-tuning, we don’t necessarily throw away RAG. And when we introduce a predictive model, we don’t necessarily replace the generative model. Instead, the architecture becomes compositional. We might ultimately have: Prompting + RAG + Fine-Tuning + Predictive ML with each component doing the job for which it is best suited. This is why the development spectrum shouldn’t be interpreted simply as a maturity ladder where every sophisticated application eventually reaches the right-hand side. The correct architecture depends on the problem. Discussion question for students At this point, I would ask: If RAG gives the model access to the company’s troubleshooting manuals, why would we still need fine-tuning? Both disciplines are concerned with getting AI systems into production reliably. They share ideas such as versioning, evaluation, monitoring, deployment controls, and observability. But the thing we're operating—and therefore what we need to control—is substantially different. The table compares those differences across five dimensions.  1. Primary artifact — What are we actually managing? Start with the first row. In traditional MLOps, the central artifact is typically the trained model—its weights, architecture, hyperparameters, and associated preprocessing logic. For our NorthStar churn model, for example, we train an XGBoost model. We produce model.tar.gz, evaluate it, register it, and ultimately deploy that specific model artifact. With LLMOps, that's often not what we're doing. If we're using a hosted foundation model, we usually don't own or train the base-model weights. For a RAG application, I would also include the retrieval index and knowledge corpus as important versioned artifacts. The second row shows another important difference. Traditional ML frequently has a significant training cost, especially as model size and dataset size increase. Once a relatively lightweight model is trained, however, individual predictions may be inexpensive. For something like NorthStar's XGBoost churn model, that pattern is particularly clear: training is a discrete job, while inference is lightweight. Hosted foundation models often reverse some of that operational emphasis. The provider has absorbed the enormous cost of pretraining the foundation model. We consume it through inference. That means our costs increasingly depend on things such as: tokens, requests, context-window size, model choice, retrieval volume, and agent/tool usage. At small scale, this may be inexpensive. At enterprise volume, inference costs can become substantial. That's why FinOps becomes tightly connected to LLMOps. Token usage and model routing aren't merely engineering details—they become production cost controls. One nuance: this row describes a common hosted-LLM pattern, not a universal rule. Fine-tuning or self-hosting foundation models can shift the cost profile significantly. 3. Evaluation — What does “correct” mean? This is probably the most important difference on the slide. For conventional predictive ML, evaluation is often based on relatively objective metrics. For classification we might use: accuracy, precision, recall, F1, or ROC-AUC. For regression we might use RMSE or MAE. And we usually have labeled ground truth against which we can compare the prediction. Our NorthStar churn model is a good example. We can ask: “Did this customer actually churn?” and compare that outcome to the prediction. With generative AI, there often isn't one correct answer. Two responses can be completely different and both be good. So LLM evaluation becomes multidimensional. We may need to evaluate: correctness, relevance, coherence, groundedness, helpfulness, safety, style, and adherence to instructions. For RAG, we may separately evaluate retrieval quality and answer faithfulness. And because many of these qualities are difficult to capture with one deterministic metric, evaluation may combine automated tests, LLM-as-judge, and human evaluation. That is why LLMOps requires a much richer evaluation framework. 4. Version control and ownership The fourth row changes the boundary of what we control. With conventional MLOps, if we've trained the model ourselves, we have substantial control over the model artifact. The advantage is that we don't have to modify the active production pipeline in place. If the new version has a problem, we still have the previous environment available. For data systems, the key question is: “Does the new pipeline produce correct and trustworthy data?” Churn model — Canary The NorthStar churn model uses a canary deployment. We initially expose the new model to perhaps 5% of traffic, evaluate AUC, latency, errors, and other relevant metrics, and then progressively increase traffic—25%, 50%, and eventually 100%. That limits our exposure to a bad model. Instead of asking “Did deployment succeed?”, we're asking: “Does this model continue to perform correctly under real production traffic?” Only then does it become the full production model. Offer generation — Shadow, then Canary The offer-generation system requires additional caution because we're dealing with generative AI. We begin in shadow mode. The candidate system receives real requests, but customers don't see its responses. We compare those outputs for quality and safety. If the candidate performs well, we can move into a canary deployment and gradually expose it to actual users. So this path becomes: Shadow → Compare → Canary → Production. This gives us real-world evidence without immediately putting generated content in front of customers. Escalation agent — Mandatory Shadow The escalation agent gets even stronger controls. Why? Because an agent can potentially take actions, not simply make predictions or generate text. So shadow mode is mandatory. We observe what the agent would have done and evaluate those actions for safety and accuracy before giving it production authority. Only after those evaluations pass do we enable the agent—with the appropriate guardrails. This illustrates an important AgentOps principle: The more authority we give an AI system, the stronger the controls around deployment should become. Infrastructure — GitOps Finally, infrastructure follows a GitOps model. Git defines our desired infrastructure state. Once a change passes CI, the infrastructure is updated, health checks validate the resulting environment, and the system reconciles actual state against the desired state. If something goes wrong, we can revert to a known-good configuration. So even though infrastructure looks different from a model or an agent, it follows the same basic philosophy: Define → Validate → Apply → Observe → Recover. 3. Production is not the end The bottom section is deliberately shared again because all five paths converge on the same operational requirement: monitoring. Once something reaches production, we continuously observe metrics, logs, alerts, data quality, model performance, and—where appropriate—user or business outcomes. There are then three basic outcomes. If everything is healthy, keep running. If something looks suspicious, alert and investigate. If a critical threshold is violated, roll back or automatically recover. That turns deployment from a one-time event into an ongoing operational process. Why this architecture is simpler The important simplification in this diagram is that we don't need to memorize dozens of individual pipeline steps. There are really only three ideas: First: one common CI pipeline.Build it, test it, secure it, and validate it before deployment. Second: deployment strategy depends on artifact risk.Data gets blue/green. Models get canary. GenAI gets shadow plus canary. Agents get mandatory shadow. Infrastructure gets GitOps. Third: everything is monitored after deployment.Production is where continuous validation begins, not where validation ends. Connection to XOps This also shows why we're talking about XOps rather than only MLOps. The green path is DataOps. The blue path is MLOps. The purple path introduces LLMOps. The orange path introduces AgentOps. And the infrastructure path draws on DevOps and GitOps. They're different disciplines, but they share the same operational philosophy: automation, testing, controlled promotion, observability, and recovery. Key takeaway The sentence I want you to remember from this slide is: Standardize the controls; specialize the deployment strategy. NorthStar doesn't need five completely independent CI/CD architectures. We can reuse a common CI foundation and then choose the appropriate deployment technique based on the artifact's behavior and risk. So the entire diagram can be reduced to: Commit → Validate → Deploy Safely → Monitor → Recover. That's the core XOps pattern—and we'll reuse it whether we're operating data pipelines, traditional ML models, generative AI systems, agents, or the infrastructure underneath them.

We validate schema and data quality, check for drift or anomalies, enforce the model-quality threshold, and verify the inference contract. We can even require the candidate model to outperform the existing production baseline. So automation doesn't mean “deploy everything faster.” It means automatically reject changes that don't meet our standards. CloudWatch — observe the pipeline itself:Finally, the pipeline is also a production system, so we need to monitor it. The CloudWatch dashboard tracks run history, success and failure rates, execution latency, training metrics, and alerts. This gives us observability not only into the deployed model, but into the machinery that builds and deploys that model. Key takeaway:Lab 4 is where NorthStar makes the transition from repeatable MLOps to automated MLOps. CodePipeline orchestrates the overall CI/CD process, CodeBuild tests the change, SageMaker Pipelines automates the ML lifecycle, the ConditionStep enforces model quality, the Model Registry versions successful models, and CloudWatch tells us whether the entire system is healthy.
