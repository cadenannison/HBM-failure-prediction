## Slide 4
- The Evaluation Framework: From Metrics to Decisions
- A framework turns measurements into an auditable go / no-go call.
- 4
> Notes: An evaluation framework has three parts, and you need all three before training starts.  Metrics are what you measure: AUC for the churn model, faithfulness for RAG, resolution rate for the agent.  Thresholds are the values that count as "good enough" to deploy.  Decision logic combines several metrics into a single go-or-no-go call.  Bottom left: the three most common mistakes.  Picking thresholds arbitrarily ("0.70 AUC sounds good").  Relying on one metric when several dimensions matter.  And having no baseline: a new model has to beat something — the current production model, or at minimum a simple rule.  Bottom right is NorthStar's churn gate logic.  It's a conjunction: AUC at least 0.72 AND  precision at least 0.18 when the threshold is set to catch 70% of churners AND  beating production by at least 2% AUC.   All three pass, we deploy. Any one fails, we alert and retrain. Notice there's no averaging here — a great AUC can't buy back a failed baseline comparison.

## Slide 5
- How NorthStar Derived Its Thresholds
- Every gate number traces back to a business requirement.
- 5
> Notes: This is how NorthStar arrived at its numbers — four steps. The previous slide showed where the business requirement itself comes from; this one turns it into a gate.  Step 1, the business requirement. The retained-revenue target says: reach at least 70% of churners before they leave, which retains roughly $630K of gross margin a year — the money left from those customers' purchases after paying for the products. And every offer has to pay for itself on average, which means precision above the break-even of about 0.13. So the requirement has two parts: a recall target that sets the revenue, and a precision floor that keeps the campaign profitable.  Step 2, translate that into a model metric. The team ran a historical analysis: with AUC at or above 0.72, setting the threshold to catch 70% of churners gives about 0.20 precision — one in five offers goes to a real churner. On the chart, that's the point on the teal 0.72 curve at a true positive rate of 0.7, which sits at a false positive rate near 0.38. As AUC rises, the curve bows toward the top left, so you reach the same 70% of churners while flagging fewer customers who'd have stayed.  A number worth checking whenever you see one: with 12% churn, "78% recall at 0.4 precision" would need an AUC near 0.9. A churn model at 0.72 can't do that. Precision depends on the base rate, so always sanity-check precision claims against how rare the positive class is.  Step 3, the safety margin. At the 0.72 gate, we expect 0.20 precision against a 0.13 break-even. If the model degrades a little in production, offers still pay for themselves. That's why the scorecard gate is precision ≥ 0.18 at 70% recall — slightly below what we expect, well above break-even.  Step 4, the baseline. A new model must also beat the current production model by at least 2% AUC — otherwise why take on the deployment risk?  This derivation is what separates professional ML engineering from toy projects. When an executive asks why the gate is 0.72, you show this analysis — not "it seemed like a good threshold." This documentation belongs in your evaluation report, which is a Lab 4 deliverable.  (Curves are illustrative, drawn with a standard binormal model at each AUC.)

- Implementing Segment Evaluation as a Gate
- Score each segment separately; skip any segment without enough churners and stayers to judge.

- from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
- def evaluate_by_segment(model, X, y, seg_col,
- min_pos=30, gate=0.60):
- results = {}
- segs = X[seg_col].fillna("Unknown")
- for seg in segs.unique():
- mask = (segs == seg).to_numpy()
- y_seg = y.to_numpy()[mask]
- n_pos = int(y_seg.sum())
- if min(n_pos, len(y_seg) - n_pos) < min_pos:
- results[seg] = ("INSUFFICIENT_DATA", None)
- continue
- X_seg = X[mask].drop(columns=[seg_col])
- p = model.predict_proba(X_seg)[:, 1]
- auc = roc_auc_score(y_seg, p)
- status = "PASS" if auc >= gate else "FAIL"
- results[seg] = (status, round(auc, 3))
- return results
- 1
- Set up: Import AUC; set the 30-case minimum and the 0.60 gate.
- 2
- Loop: Blanks become "Unknown"; visit each segment.
- 3
- Slice: True/False mask; outcomes as a NumPy array.
- 4
- Guard: Under 30 churners or 30 stayers → INSUFFICIENT_DATA.
- 5
- Score: Drop the label; churn probabilities → AUC.
- 6
- Judge: PASS if ≥ 0.60, else FAIL; store status + AUC.
- Enforce → next step: lowest segment goes to evaluation.json. See appendix.

> Notes: Here's the segment evaluation function. It answers one question: does the churn model work well enough for every customer segment, not just on average? Each colored band on the left matches a numbered step on the right.  1. Set up. Import scikit-learn's AUC function and define the inputs: the model; the test features X as a DataFrame that also holds a customer_segment column; the true outcomes y (1 = churned, 0 = stayed); the name of the segment column; the minimum count we need before we trust an AUC (30); and the gate (0.60).  2. Clean and loop. results starts as an empty dictionary. fillna("Unknown") gives customers with a blank segment their own group — without it, blank values would silently drop out of every comparison and never be evaluated. Then the loop visits each segment name once.  3. Slice. mask is a True/False array marking this segment's customers. Converting it with to_numpy(), and taking y as a NumPy array too, matches rows by position. That avoids a subtle pandas bug: if y's index doesn't line up with X's, label-based indexing can pull the wrong outcomes.  4. Guard. n_pos counts the churners (outcomes are 1s and 0s, so the sum is the count); len(y_seg) − n_pos is the stayers. AUC compares churners against stayers, so we need at least 30 of each. Fewer, and the segment is recorded as INSUFFICIENT_DATA and skipped with continue. This also prevents a crash: roc_auc_score raises an error if a segment has only one class.  5. Score. Drop the segment label so the model sees exactly its training features, take the churn probability with predict_proba(...)[:, 1], and compute AUC within the segment. One assumption: the segment label was not a training feature; if it was, don't drop it.  6. Judge. 0.60 or above is PASS, below is FAIL. Each segment's result is a (status, AUC) pair, so the report shows the number, not just the verdict. The function returns the whole dictionary — for v3.1, High-Value PASS 0.812, Low-Value New FAIL 0.58, Seasonal-B INSUFFICIENT_DATA.  The dashed box: enforcement now lives in the pipeline, not in an assert. Python skips assert statements when run with the -O flag, so a pipeline shouldn't rely on them. On the next slide, evaluate.py writes the worst segment AUC to evaluation.json, and the ConditionStep blocks registration if it's below 0.60. In other words, it saves the lowest segment AUC to evaluation.json, and the pipeline stops if it’s below 0.60.

## Slide 11
- A/B Testing for ML Systems
- Validate a challenger on real traffic — with consistent assignment and enough time to see outcomes.
- 11
> Notes: A/B testing an ML model isn't the same as A/B testing a button color. Three differences.  One: consistent assignment. If a customer sees Model A today and Model B tomorrow, their outcome is contaminated by both, and you can't attribute anything. So, we split by customer, not by request or session.  Two: the evaluation window has to be long enough to observe the outcome. Churn plays out over weeks, not hours.  Three: business metrics lag model metrics. The model emits a churn probability immediately; we don't know whether the customer actually churned until much later.  The architecture: a request comes in, the splitter hashes customer_id and takes it mod 5. Buckets 0 through 3 go to Model A, the control — that's 80%. Bucket 4 goes to Model B, the challenger — 20%. Because it's a hash, the same customer always lands in the same bucket. Both endpoints log their predictions; the evaluation system joins those logs with actual churn outcomes after the 4-week window, runs the significance test, and only then do we promote B.

> Notes: Here's the concrete design for the churn model test.  Setup: Model A is the current production model, v2.3. Model B is the challenger, v3.0. The split is 80/20 — deliberately asymmetric. And assignment is by customer_id hash, so a customer always sees the same model.  The asymmetric split is a risk-management decision. You're not running this test to maximize statistical power; you're running it to validate the new model with minimal exposure in case it turns out to be worse. Once you're confident the new model is better, shift to 50/50, then to 100% if the results hold. Never go straight to 100% without validation.  Window: four weeks minimum, because churn events take two to three weeks to manifest. The success metric is the observed churn rate in the control population versus the challenger population.  Statistical criteria — four numbers that go into a power analysis. - Minimum detectable effect, 10% relative: the smallest improvement the test is built to catch. Relative means 10% of the current rate: churn falling from 12.0% to 10.8%, a 1.2-point drop. It's a business choice: the smallest gain worth deploying a new model for. - 80% power: if v3.0 really is at least that much better, the test has an 80% chance of showing it. The other 20% is the risk of a false negative — missing a real improvement. - p < 0.05, two-tailed: if the models were actually identical, a difference this large would show up by chance less than 5% of the time. That caps false positives at about 1 in 20. Two-tailed means we'd also detect it if v3.0 were worse — which is exactly what we want to know before deploying. - The output: about 6,700 customers in the challenger group and 26,800 in control, roughly 33,500 in total.  Two teaching points. First, the 80/20 split has a price: with a 50/50 split you'd need only about 11,000 per arm, 22,000 in total. The small arm limits the test, so protecting customers from an unproven model costs about 50% more samples. Second, why not aim for a 5% effect? Detecting 12.0% → 11.4% would need about 28,000 customers in B and 111,000 in A — more customers than NorthStar has. Halving the effect you want to detect roughly quadruples the sample you need.  Quick check for the class: "Leadership insists on detecting a 5% improvement. What are your options?" (Switch to 50/50, run longer to accumulate customers, or accept that gains that small can't be measured reliably.)

- 15% effect

> Notes: POWER ANALYSIS. Before running an A/B test, we have to decide how many customers it needs. Too few, and a real improvement looks like noise. Too many, and we expose customers to an unproven model longer than necessary. A power analysis answers the question before the test starts.  Power analysis estimates how many samples you need to reliably detect a meaningful model improvement rather than mistake random variation for progress. It uses three main considerations: Improvement size: Smaller improvements require more samples. Variability: Noisier results require more samples. Confidence and power: Stronger protection against false alarms and a higher chance of detecting a real improvement require more samples. The result is a target sample size for the evaluation—not a guarantee that the model will improve.   Left side: four inputs, one answer. 1. Baseline rate — what happens today. NorthStar's churn is 12%. 2. The smallest effect worth finding — the minimum detectable effect. We chose a 10% relative improvement: churn falling from 12.0% to 10.8%. This is a business decision: what's the smallest gain worth deploying a new model for? 3. Significance level, alpha = 0.05. If the models are really identical, we accept a 5% chance of wrongly declaring a winner — a false positive, about 1 in 20. 4. Power, 80%. If the improvement is real, we want an 80% chance of detecting it — 4 times in 5. The other 20% is the false-negative risk. Plug those in, and you get about 11,000 customers per arm with a 50/50 split. With our 80/20 split, the small arm limits the test, so it takes about 6,700 in the challenger group and 26,800 in the control group.  Top right: the formula for two proportions. The z-values come from the normal distribution: 1.96 for a two-tailed 5% significance level and 0.84 for 80% power. "Noise" is the variance of each group's churn rate — p times 1 minus p for each arm, 0.106 plus 0.096. "Effect" is the difference we want to detect — 1.2 points, or 0.012. Squared, that's tiny, which is why the sample is large. You don't need to memorize this; the point is the shape: the effect is squared in the denominator.  Bottom right: that squared effect is the single most important intuition. Halve the effect you want to detect and you need about four times the sample. A 15% effect needs about 4,800 per arm; 10% needs about 11,000; 5% needs about 45,000 — more customers than NorthStar has if most must stay on the control model.  In code, statsmodels does this for you:   from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower   from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_effectsize   es = proportion_effectsize(0.12, 0.108)   NormalIndPower().solve_power(es, alpha=0.05, power=0.80, ratio=1) That returns about 11,000. With ratio=4 for an 80/20 split it returns about 6,900 for the small arm — slightly different from our 6,700 because statsmodels uses a different approximation. Either is fine for planning.  Three rules of practice: - Do it before the test, and stick to the number. Stopping as soon as p < 0.05 — peeking — breaks the guarantees you just calculated. - If the required sample is too big, your options are: accept a larger minimum effect, run the test longer, use a more even split, or pick a metric that moves more. - Under-powered tests are worse than useless. With only 2,000 customers per arm, this test would have about 22% power — it would miss a real 10% improvement nearly four times out of five, and the team would conclude "the new model doesn't help."  Quick check for the class: "Marketing wants an answer in half the time, with half the customers. What happens to the smallest effect you can detect?" (It grows by about 1.4×, the square root of 2 — from 10% to roughly 14%.)

## Slide 20
- RAGAS Deep Dive: What the Metrics Measure
- Each metric isolates a different failure point in the RAG pipeline.
- Faithfulness
- ≥ 0.95
- Are the answer’s claims supported by the retrieved context?
- How it’s measured
- Split the answer into atomic claims → check each against the context → supported ÷ total.
- NorthStar example
- Offer says “you bought Nike shoes last month” — is that in the retrieved history?
- Pitfall: grounded ≠ correct. Garbage in, garbage out.
- Answer Relevancy
- ≥ 0.85
- Does the answer respond to what was asked?
- How it’s measured
- Generate synthetic questions from the answer → embedding similarity to the original question.
- NorthStar example
- Customer asked about shoes; the offer pitches kitchen appliances → low relevancy.
- High score: the offer addresses what the customer wants.
- Context Recall
- ≥ 0.80
- Did retrieval find the relevant information?
- How it’s measured
- Compare retrieved contexts with the ground-truth relevant documents.
- NorthStar example
- Recent running-shoe buyer, but running-shoe offers weren’t retrieved → low recall.
- High score: retrieval is capturing the right documents.
- 20
> Notes: Three RAGAS metrics, each isolating a different failure point.  Faithfulness asks: what fraction of the claims in the answer are supported by the retrieved context? RAGAS decomposes the answer into atomic claims, verifies each against the retrieved contexts, and divides supported claims by total claims. NorthStar example: the offer says "you purchased Nike shoes last month" — does that claim appear in the retrieved customer history? The pitfall: high faithfulness doesn't mean the response is correct. It means it's grounded in whatever was retrieved. If retrieval pulled the wrong customer's history, you can be perfectly faithful and completely wrong.  Answer relevancy asks: does the answer actually respond to the question? RAGAS generates synthetic questions from the answer and compares them to the original question with embedding similarity. Example: the customer asked for shoe recommendations; the offer is about kitchen appliances — low relevancy.  Context recall asks: did retrieval actually find the relevant information? It compares retrieved contexts against ground-truth relevant documents. Example: a customer recently bought running shoes, but the system never retrieved running-shoe category offers.  Use them together diagnostically: low recall points to retrieval; low faithfulness with good recall points to generation.

- 0.000
- Lower is better; 0 is perfect.
- Compare it to the always-guess-12% score (0.106), not to 0.25.

- 24
> Notes: The calibration curve on the last slide is a picture. The Brier score turns probability quality into a single number we can put on a scorecard and track over time.  The formula is simple: for each customer, take the predicted probability, subtract what actually happened — 1 if they churned, 0 if they didn't — square the difference, and average over everyone. Lower is better; zero would mean the model predicted 1.0 for every churner and 0.0 for everyone else.  Walk the five-customer example. A churned and the model said 0.80: the gap is 0.2, squared 0.04 — a small penalty. B stayed and the model said 0.10: 0.01. C stayed but got 0.30: 0.09. D churned but the model said only 0.40: the gap is 0.6, squared 0.36 — by far the biggest penalty. E stayed at 0.20: 0.04. The average is 0.108. Squaring means confident mistakes hurt much more than small ones.  The top right is the part students usually get wrong: what counts as a good score? You'll often read that 0.25 is the "no skill" benchmark — that's what you get by guessing 0.5 for everyone. But with 12% churn, there's a much smarter dumb model: predict 12% for every customer. Its Brier score is 0.12 × 0.88 ≈ 0.106. Any useful churn model has to beat 0.106, not 0.25.  A well-calibrated model at our AUC of 0.72 lands around 0.097 — better than the base-rate guess, but not by a lot. That's normal for churn: individual behavior is noisy, so even good probabilities are uncertain.  Now the red bar. This model ranks customers exactly as well as the calibrated one — same AUC — but its scores are inflated: it predicts about 36% churn on average when the real rate is 12%. Its Brier score is about 0.165, far worse than just guessing 12%. That's why we use both metrics: AUC can't see this problem, but Brier can.  Takeaways at the bottom right: - Lower is better. - Judge a Brier score against the base-rate guess for your data, not against 0.25. A common way to express that is the Brier skill score: 1 minus the model's Brier divided by the base-rate Brier. For the calibrated model, 1 − 0.097 / 0.106 ≈ 0.08 — about 8% better than guessing the average. - Brier mixes two things: calibration (do the probabilities match reality?) and ranking (does the model separate churners from stayers?). If Brier gets worse, look at the calibration curve and ECE to see whether the scores drifted or the ranking got weaker. If it's calibration, the fix is cheap: Platt scaling or isotonic regression on the next slide.  A note on terms: people sometimes say "Brier calibration," but the Brier score isn't a pure calibration measure. It's an overall accuracy score for probabilities, and calibration is one component. ## Slide 26
- Five Evaluation Mistakes That Sink Deployments
- Each one makes a model look better on paper than it will be in production.
- 1
- Test-set contamination
- Split by time first, then engineer features inside each split
- 2
- Wrong metric for the problem
- With 88% non-churners, accuracy misleads; use AUC + P/R at threshold
- 3
- Single-split evaluation
- Use 3-fold time-series cross-validation, not one holdout
- 4
- Ignoring the deployment distribution
- Evaluate on recent data: the last 6 months at most
- 5
- No baseline comparison
- Beat production and a simple rule (no purchase in 60 days)
- Mistake 5, made visible
- +0.05 AUC over a simple rule is the number that justifies deployment.
- 26
> Notes: Five mistakes that lead to failed deployments.  One: test-set contamination. Splitting after feature engineering — especially time-series features — leaks future information into training. This is the silent killer of student ML projects. You engineer RFM features on the full dataset, then split. But the recency feature for the test set was computed using purchase dates that include purchases after the split date. The model learned from the future. Fix: always split first, then engineer features within each split.  Two: the wrong metric. Churn data is imbalanced — 88% of customers don't churn — so a model that predicts "no churn" for everyone is 88% accurate and useless. Use AUC for rank-ordering quality, plus precision and recall at the intervention threshold.  Three: single-split evaluation. One holdout set gives high-variance results. For NorthStar, use three-fold time-series cross-validation: each fold trains on an earlier period and validates on the next.  Four: ignoring the deployment distribution. Evaluating on data from two years ago when you'll be scoring today's customers. Behavior changes; use at most the last six months.  Five: no baseline. "AUC is 0.74" means nothing in isolation. Compare to production and to a simple rule like "predict churn if no purchase in 60 days." Here the rule scores 0.69 and XGBoost 0.74 — a 0.05 gain, about 7% relative. That's the meaningful comparison. A model that beats the gate but not a simple rule isn't worth deploying.

## Slide 38
- Implementing Segment Evaluation as a Gate
- Score each segment separately; skip any segment without enough churners and stayers to judge.
- from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
- def evaluate_by_segment(model, X, y, seg_col,
- min_pos=30, gate=0.60):
- results = {}
- segs = X[seg_col].fillna("Unknown")
- for seg in segs.unique():
- mask = (segs == seg).to_numpy()
- y_seg = y.to_numpy()[mask]
- n_pos = int(y_seg.sum())
- if min(n_pos, len(y_seg) - n_pos) < min_pos:
- results[seg] = ("INSUFFICIENT_DATA", None)
- continue
- X_seg = X[mask].drop(columns=[seg_col])
- p = model.predict_proba(X_seg)[:, 1]
- auc = roc_auc_score(y_seg, p)
- status = "PASS" if auc >= gate else "FAIL"
- results[seg] = (status, round(auc, 3))
- return results
- One AUC per segment
- Same model, scored separately on each customer_segment value.
- Small-sample guard
- Fewer than 30 churners or 30 stayers → INSUFFICIENT_DATA, not a noisy pass or fail.
- Safe slicing
- Blank segments become "Unknown"; NumPy masks avoid index mix-ups.
- 38
> Notes: Here's the segment evaluation function. It answers one question: does the churn model work well enough for every customer segment, not just on average? It returns a verdict per segment; the next slide turns those verdicts into a pipeline gate.  Walk it top to bottom.  The inputs: the model, the test features X as a DataFrame that also holds a customer_segment column, the true outcomes y (1 = churned, 0 = stayed), the name of the segment column, the minimum count we need before we trust an AUC (30), and the gate (0.60).  segs = X[seg_col].fillna("Unknown"). Customers with a blank segment get their own "Unknown" group. Without this, blank values would silently drop out of every comparison and never be evaluated.  The loop visits each segment name once. mask is a True/False array marking that segment's customers. We convert it with to_numpy(), and take y as a NumPy array too, so rows are matched by position. That avoids a subtle pandas bug: if y's index doesn't line up with X's, label-based indexing can pull the wrong outcomes.  The guard counts churners (n_pos) and stayers (the rest) and requires at least 30 of each. AUC compares churners against stayers, so it's unreliable if either group is small — and roc_auc_score raises an error if a segment has only one class. Segments that fail the guard are recorded as INSUFFICIENT_DATA and skipped with continue.  For segments with enough data: drop the segment label so the model sees exactly its training features, get churn probabilities with predict_proba(...)[:, 1], compute AUC within the segment, and mark PASS if it's at least 0.60, FAIL otherwise. Each segment's result is a (status, AUC) pair, so the report can show the number as well as the verdict.  One assumption to state: the segment label must not have been a training feature. If it was, don't drop it before predicting.  Notice there's no assert here anymore. Python skips assert statements when run with the -O flag, so a pipeline shouldn't rely on them. The next slide shows the enforcement: evaluate.py writes the worst segment AUC to evaluation.json, and the ConditionStep blocks registration if it's below 0.60.

## Slide 41
- Power Analysis: How Many Customers Do We Need?
- 41
- An arm means one experimental group: A is the control; B receives the intervention. Power is conditional on the assumed effect—it does not mean an 80% probability that the intervention works.
> Notes: POWER ANALYSIS. Before running an A/B test, we have to decide how many customers it needs. Too few, and a real improvement looks like noise. Too many, and we expose customers to an unproven model longer than necessary. A power analysis answers the question before the test starts.  Left side: four inputs, one answer. 1. Baseline rate — what happens today. NorthStar's churn is 12%. 2. The smallest effect worth finding — the minimum detectable effect. We chose a 10% relative improvement: churn falling from 12.0% to 10.8%. This is a business decision: what's the smallest gain worth deploying a new model for? 3. Significance level, alpha = 0.05. If the models are really identical, we accept a 5% chance of wrongly declaring a winner — a false positive, about 1 in 20. 4. Power, 80%. If the improvement is real, we want an 80% chance of detecting it — 4 times in 5. The other 20% is the false-negative risk. Plug those in, and you get about 11,000 customers per arm with a 50/50 split. With our 80/20 split, the small arm limits the test, so it takes about 6,700 in the challenger group and 26,800 in the control group.  Top right: the formula for two proportions. The z-values come from the normal distribution: 1.96 for a two-tailed 5% significance level and 0.84 for 80% power. "Noise" is the variance of each group's churn rate — p times 1 minus p for each arm, 0.106 plus 0.096. "Effect" is the difference we want to detect — 1.2 points, or 0.012. Squared, that's tiny, which is why the sample is large. You don't need to memorize this; the point is the shape: the effect is squared in the denominator.  Bottom right: that squared effect is the single most important intuition. Halve the effect you want to detect and you need about four times the sample. A 15% effect needs about 4,800 per arm; 10% needs about 11,000; 5% needs about 45,000 — more customers than NorthStar has if most must stay on the control model.  In code, statsmodels does this for you:   from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower   from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_effectsize   es = proportion_effectsize(0.12, 0.108)   NormalIndPower().solve_power(es, alpha=0.05, power=0.80, ratio=1) That returns about 11,000. With ratio=4 for an 80/20 split it returns about 6,900 for the small arm — slightly different from our 6,700 because statsmodels uses a different approximation. Either is fine for planning.  Three rules of practice: - Do it before the test, and stick to the number. Stopping as soon as p < 0.05 — peeking — breaks the guarantees you just calculated. - If the required sample is too big, your options are: accept a larger minimum effect, run the test longer, use a more even split, or pick a metric that moves more. - Under-powered tests are worse than useless. With only 2,000 customers per arm, this test would have about 22% power — it would miss a real 10% improvement nearly four times out of five, and the team would conclude "the new model doesn't help." > Notes: Two evaluation modes, and you need both.  Offline evaluation happens before deployment, on held-out historical data. It's fast — no live traffic required — and it answers: how would this model have performed on past data? Its blind spots: it can't capture distribution shift between your historical data and today's production traffic, and it can't measure downstream business outcomes. NorthStar examples: AUC on the holdout set, RAGAS on a curated test set, and agent simulation tests.  Online evaluation happens after deployment, on real production traffic. It's delayed — actual churn takes weeks to show up. It answers: how is the model actually performing for real users today? Its strength: it captures the true distribution and real business impact. NorthStar examples: actual churn rate in the intervention cohort versus control, CSAT on offer responses, and agent resolution rate.  The relationship: offline evaluation is necessary but not sufficient. Passing offline earns you the right to be evaluated online.
