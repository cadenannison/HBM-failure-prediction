> Notes: STRIDE is a threat-modeling mnemonic from Microsoft. It gives a team a structured way to ask “what could go wrong?” about a system while it’s still being designed. Origins. Loren Kohnfelder and Praerit Garg created it at Microsoft in 1999, in an internal paper called “The Threats to Our Products.” It became central to Microsoft’s Security Development Lifecycle (SDL) after the 2002 Trustworthy Computing push, which required threat modeling before products shipped. Adam Shostack, who led much of Microsoft’s threat-modeling work, popularized it further in his 2014 book Threat Modeling: Designing for Security.  How it’s used. The classic method is STRIDE-per-element: Draw a data-flow diagram of the system (processes, data stores, data flows, external entities and trust boundaries). Go through each element and ask which STRIDE threats apply to it. Record each threat with a mitigation, which gives you the familiar threat-model table.  Strengths and limits. STRIDE is simple, easy to teach, and good at making sure no category gets forgotten. However: The categories overlap. One attack can fit several letters, as the prompt injection row on slide 2 shows. It doesn’t rank risk. Microsoft paired it with a scoring scheme called DREAD, then later dropped DREAD as too subjective. Teams now usually rate risk separately, for example with CVSS or a simple likelihood-and-impact grid. It wasn’t built for privacy or AI. That gap led to related frameworks: LINDDUN for privacy threats, and MITRE ATLAS and the OWASP LLM and Agentic Top 10 lists for AI-specific attacks. Teams often use STRIDE as the backbone and these lists to fill in AI threats.

## Slide 11
- STRIDE Threat Model for AI Systems
- Three rows that are new for AI
> Notes: What does a STRIDE threat model look like when the system includes a model, and which threats are new?  STRIDE is the classic Microsoft threat taxonomy, adapted for AI. Its output is a list of threats with likelihood and impact, owners and planned mitigations.  Spoofing: impersonating a trusted model, API, data source or another agent; mitigate with strong identity, mTLS and IAM roles per service.   mTLS (mutual TLS) is TLS where both sides prove their identity with certificates, not just the server. Ordinary TLS versus mTLS Ordinary TLS (HTTPS): only the server shows a certificate. Your browser checks that it’s really talking to bank.com, but the server has no cryptographic proof of who the client is. The client usually identifies itself later, with a password, an API key or a token. mTLS: during the handshake, the server also asks for a client certificate. The client presents one and proves it holds the matching private key. The server checks that the certificate was issued by a certificate authority it trusts. If any check fails, the connection never opens. What it buys you Strong machine identity. Possession of a private key replaces shared secrets like API keys, which leak through code, logs and config files. Encryption and integrity in transit, just as with ordinary TLS. Access control at the connection. Unknown clients are rejected before they reach application code. In STRIDE terms, it mainly addresses Spoofing, with help on Tampering and Information disclosure for data in transit.  Tampering: a poisoned dataset or retrieval corpus, a modified model or pipeline; mitigate with provenance, signing, and versioning.  Repudiation: disputed model or tool actions; mitigate with tamper-evident logs, meaning CloudTrail with log file validation.  Information disclosure: extraction of training data, PII, or the system prompt; mitigate with output filters, differential privacy, and returning the least information needed.  Denial of service now includes cost exhaustion, or "denial of wallet"; mitigate with rate limits, per-user token budgets, autoscaling, circuit breakers, and API Gateway throttling. Elevation of privilege includes indirect injection and the confused deputy; mitigate with per-user permissions and minimal tools.  A confused deputy is a program that holds legitimate privileges and gets tricked into using them for someone who shouldn’t have them. The attacker never breaks in or steals credentials. They just ask the “deputy” to do something, and the deputy does it with its own authority instead of the requester’s. AI agent example: an agent running under a broadly privileged service account will act for whoever talks to it, including through a prompt injection.  The three rows on the right are the ones that change most for AI. These are new. We’ve talked about a lot of different threat and attack types. How many do you think?

## Slide 16
- CI Security Scanning
> Notes: How do we catch security problems automatically before risky code reaches main?  The idea is a pipeline of gates. Every commit goes through automated checks, and if a required check fails, the merge is blocked. Nobody needs to remember to run a scan, because the pipeline runs it every time.  The flow (top row).  Pre-commit checks run on the developer’s machine. Secret-scanning hooks catch an API key before it’s ever committed. Push protection on the Git host is the server-side backstop that rejects a push containing a known secret pattern.  CI scans run on every pull request, no matter what ran locally. Pre-commit hooks can be skipped or never installed, so CI is the gate you can actually enforce.  Required checks make the result binding. The scan has to be configured as a required status check in branch protection. Otherwise it’s just a warning people learn to ignore.  What CI checks (the table). Go through it as layers of the stack:  Secrets: credentials in code or history. Gitleaks and TruffleHog.  SAST: insecure patterns in our own Python, such as eval, subprocess with shell=True, or hardcoded passwords. Bandit and Semgrep.  Dependencies: known CVEs in the packages we import. Dependabot and Snyk.  Model artifacts: the ML-specific row. Pickle-based model files can run arbitrary code when they’re loaded. Downloading a model from a hub and calling pickle.load or torch.load on it is the same as running a stranger’s script. ModelScan and picklescan inspect the file without loading it.  Containers: vulnerable OS packages and libraries in the image. Trivy, or ECR’s built-in scanning.  Infrastructure as code: misconfigurations such as public S3 buckets or wildcard IAM policies, caught before deployment. Checkov and Trivy.  Lab 5, Task 5. ## Slide 18
- Red-Teaming + Augmented Regression Testing
> Notes: How do we find out how an AI system can be made to misbehave, and make sure the same failure never comes back?  The subtitle sums up the method: attack the system on purpose, and turn every confirmed failure into a test that runs on every future change. It’s the same discipline as fixing a bug and adding a unit test, applied to model behavior.  Red-teaming is authorized adversarial testing: a team plays the attacker to find weaknesses before real attackers do.  Where the name comes from. It comes from military war-gaming, especially in the Cold War, where the “red” side played the opposing force and the “blue” side the home team.   Security borrowed the colors: Red team: attacks. Blue team: defends and detects. Purple team: the two working together, so every attack becomes a better detection.  In traditional security, a red team acts like a real adversary from start to finish: phishing, getting in, moving sideways through the network, reaching a goal, all while trying to stay hidden. That’s different from a penetration test, which is usually narrower and announced, with the goal of finding as many vulnerabilities as possible in a set scope. A red team tests whether the organization notices and responds, not just whether bugs exist.  In AI, the term has broadened to mean deliberately probing a model or AI system for failures: Jailbreaks and prompt injection (direct and indirect). Leaks of the system prompt, training data or other users’ data. Agents that can be talked into harmful actions, such as the $40 + $40 credit case. Harmful, biased or false outputs. It’s done both by hand, where people write creative attacks, and with automated tools that generate attack prompts at scale, such as Microsoft’s PyRIT, NVIDIA’s garak and promptfoo. Model developers red-team models before release, and the EU AI Act requires adversarial testing for the most capable general-purpose models.  Its limit: red-teaming shows that flaws exist. It can’t show that none remain. That’s why L18 argued for designing as if the injection succeeds, using the Rule of Two and mediated actions, instead of relying on red-teaming or filters to catch every attack.   What to challenge. Four attack categories:  Prompt injection: untrusted content overriding the system’s instructions. The dangerous version is indirect injection, where the attack hides in a retrieved document, web page, or email the model reads, not in what the user types. RAG systems and agents are especially exposed.  Jailbreaks: prompts that get past safety constraints through role-play, encodings, or multi-turn pressure. The difference from injection: a jailbreak attacks the model’s safety training, while injection attacks your application’s instructions.  Data extraction: getting the system to reveal its system prompt, other users’ data, retrieved documents the user shouldn’t see, or memorized training data. This ties back to our privacy slides.  Agent boundaries: the highest-stakes category. Once a model can call tools, a successful injection becomes an action: sending email, deleting records, spending money. The question is whether tools can act beyond their authorized scope, and the real defense is least-privilege permissions, not a better prompt.  How findings improve the release gate.  Discover a failure in a controlled environment, never against production users or systems you don’t own.  Mitigate the cause. The order of the options matters. Prompt changes are the weakest fix, because a rephrased attack often gets around them. Controls such as input/output filters, and tool permissions, hold up better. Fix at the deepest layer you can.  Add a regression test. Record the exact attack and the expected safe behavior, not the exact output text.  Run in CI and block the merge if the failure comes back. This connects to the last slide: red-team regressions become another required check next to the security scans.  One thing to emphasize: LLM outputs are nondeterministic. A test that passes once can fail on the next run. Regression tests usually run each attack several times and use a grader (rules or an LLM judge) with a pass-rate threshold, not exact string matching.  Tools. promptfoo is a CLI for evals and red-teaming that fits naturally into CI. garak, from NVIDIA, is a vulnerability scanner that runs a library of probes against a model. PyRIT, from Microsoft, is a framework for orchestrating multi-turn red-team attacks.  Bottom line (read it aloud): “Red-teaming explores new failures; regression tests check known ones.” You need both. Red-teaming without regression tests means fixed bugs come back. Regression tests without red-teaming only protect you against attacks you’ve already seen.

## Slide 19
- Privacy Engineering: Three Core Principles
> Notes: What does it actually take to make an ML system protect privacy, instead of just promising to? Privacy isn’t a policy document you attach after the system is built. It’s a property of the architecture, and technical controls enforce it. Here are three principles, each covering a different stage of the pipeline.  First, minimize and limit purpose. This happens before any data reaches the model. Use the smallest feature set that meets the requirement. Every extra column is extra risk, and it often adds little lift. Then enforce permitted use mechanically. Classify the data, tag columns as PII or sensitive, and use access controls so a dataset collected for fraud detection can’t quietly be reused for marketing. If purpose limitation only exists as a rule people are asked to follow, expect it to be broken eventually.  Second, de-identify, and know which kind you did. Teams get this wrong all the time. Anonymized data can’t reasonably be linked back to a person, so regulations like GDPR no longer apply to it. That status has to be earned. You assess the re-identification risk, including linkage attacks that combine your data with outside datasets, and you document the assessment. Pseudonymized data replaces direct identifiers with tokens, but anyone holding the mapping table can reverse it. Legally, it’s still personal data. The main control is to keep the mapping table separate from the training data, with its own access controls.  Third, bound what training can reveal. Models memorize, and a trained model can leak individual records through membership inference or extraction attacks. Differential privacy, usually implemented as DP-SGD, clips each example’s gradient and adds calibrated noise. That puts a mathematical limit on how much any one record can influence the model. Epsilon is the privacy budget: a smaller epsilon means stronger privacy, and usually some loss of accuracy. Picking epsilon is a business decision, not only a technical one.  Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) is the gold-standard algorithm for training machine learning and deep learning models with formal privacy guarantees. Originally introduced by Abadi et al. in 2016, DP-SGD ensures that a trained model's parameters do not memorize or leak private information about individual training data points. It accomplishes this by modifying standard Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) to strictly bound the mathematical influence of any single record. > Notes: If we strip the names out of a dataset, is it still personal data?  Start with the top row: one NorthStar customer record with direct identifiers (ID, name, email) and quasi-identifiers (ZIP code and age). Quasi-identifiers do not name anyone on their own, but combined with outside data they often do. A well-known study found that ZIP code, birth date, and sex alone single out most Americans.  Left side, pseudonymization. We replace the direct identifiers with a token, usually a keyed hash such as HMAC with a secret key, or a random token in a lookup table. The secret or the table is the "additional information" GDPR Article 4(5) talks about, and it must be stored separately and protected. Three technical consequences follow. It is reversible: whoever holds the key can re-link the token to the person. It is linkable: the same customer gets the same token in every table, which is exactly why it is useful for joins and model training. And the quasi-identifiers are untouched, so a ZIP plus an age can still point to one person. Legally, pseudonymized data is still personal data, which is why our classification slide put pseudonymized training copies in the Restricted tier.  Right side, anonymization. We drop the identifiers and transform the quasi-identifiers: generalize age into bands, coarsen ZIP to a region, aggregate into groups, suppress small groups, or add calibrated noise with differential privacy. The goal is that nobody, using means reasonably likely to be used, can get back to the person. That is the Recital 26 test, and it applies to everyone, not just to us. The European regulators' standard checks are three questions: can someone be singled out, can records about the same person be linked, and can something about a person be inferred? If the answer to all three is no, the data falls outside GDPR.  The trade-off is utility. Anonymized data cannot train a per-customer churn model, because there are no per-customer rows. That is why most ML pipelines run on pseudonymized data and carry the controls that come with it.  One common mistake to call out: hashing an email address without a secret key is not anonymization. Anyone can hash a list of known emails and match them. It is weak pseudonymization at best.  Ask the room: our Internal tier holds "aggregates that passed a re-identification check." Which of the three tests would a table of average spend by ZIP code fail, if one ZIP has only two customers? Start with the top row: one NorthStar customer record with direct identifiers in red (ID, name, email) and quasi-identifiers in purple (ZIP code and age). Quasi-identifiers do not name anyone on their own, but combined with outside data they often do. A well-known study found that ZIP code, birth date, and sex alone single out most Americans.  Left panel, pseudonymization. We replace the direct identifiers with a token, usually a keyed hash such as HMAC with a secret key, or a random token in a lookup table. The key or table is the "additional information" GDPR Article 4(5) describes, and it must be stored separately. Three consequences follow. It is reversible: whoever holds the key can re-link the person. It is linkable: the same customer gets the same token in every table, which is why it works for joins and training. And the quasi-identifiers are untouched. Legally, it is still personal data, which is why pseudonymized training copies stay in the Restricted tier.  Middle panel, anonymization. We drop the identifiers and transform the quasi-identifiers: age into bands, ZIP into a region, rows into groups, small groups suppressed, or noise added with differential privacy. The Recital 26 test is whether anyone, using means reasonably likely to be used, can get back to the person. European regulators check three things: singling out, linkability, and inference. If the data passes, it falls outside GDPR. The cost is utility: there are no per-customer rows left to train a churn model on.  Right panel, synthetic data. We train a generative model on the real table, such as a Gaussian copula, a Bayesian network, or CTGAN, and sample new rows from it. Customer S-0412 does not exist, but the dataset as a whole keeps the real correlations, so you can still train on it.  Here is the catch. The generator never sees the population, only our sample. A model that matched the sample exactly would just reproduce our customers. The closer the fit, the more it leaks: an overfit generator can output a near-copy of an unusual real customer, and the membership-inference attacks from Thursday work against generators too, because a generator is a model.  So synthetic data is not automatically anonymous. Training the generator is itself processing personal data, and the output counts as anonymous only if it passes the same three tests. The strongest approach is differentially private synthesis, which caps how much any one person can affect the output. Otherwise, teams check empirically: no synthetic row should sit unusually close to a real one, and membership-inference tests should fail. For utility, the standard check is train on synthetic, test on real.  One common mistake to call out: hashing an email address without a secret key is not anonymization. Anyone can hash a list of known emails and match them.  The one-line summary: pseudonymization hides who the person is; anonymization throws away individual detail; synthetic data keeps individual detail without real individuals, but only if the generator did not memorize them. A GDPR erasure request is not one delete. The same customer lives in four places, and each one needs a different operation. Raw S3: rewrite the shared files without the customer's rows, then delete the old object versions. Feature Store: delete the online record, then rewrite the offline history. The online store holds only the latest record for each customer, for low-latency lookups at inference time. The offline store is append-only history in S3, used to build training sets. Nothing in the offline store is ever overwritten.  Walk through an example. Customer C00123456 has three feature writes: September 1, October 1, and November 1. The online store holds only the November 1 row. The offline store holds all three. The customer asks to be erased on November 10.  Online deletion is the code on the slide. Callout 1: the EventTime must be the time of the deletion, later than the stored record's own EventTime. If it is not, the deletion does not happen. With SoftDelete, that failure is silent: the record stays in the online store, but a deletion marker is still written offline, so your records say "deleted" while the endpoint keeps serving the data. With HardDelete, the failure is loud: a 400 ValidationException.  Callout 2: SoftDelete is the default, and it only sets the feature columns to null; a stub keyed by the customer ID remains. HardDelete removes the whole record. For erasure, use HardDelete, partly because its failures are visible.  Callout 3 is the trap. delete_record deletes nothing from the offline store. The current snapshot no longer shows the customer, but older snapshots still contain the rows, and Iceberg time travel can query them. Second, OPTIMIZE with REWRITE DATA, which writes new data files without those rows. Third, VACUUM, which expires old snapshots and removes the files they referenced. ## Slide 28
- Four Frameworks Impacting AI Governance
> Notes: When a customer or regulator asks “how do you govern your AI system?”, which frameworks are they actually talking about, and how do they differ?  These four get mentioned together all the time, but they aren’t the same kind of thing. The right-hand column shows the difference. One is a law, one is an audit report, one is a certifiable standard, and one is voluntary guidance. Don’t confuse what’s required with what’s chosen.  HIPAA is U.S. law. It applies to covered entities (providers, health plans, and clearinghouses) and to their business associates, the vendors that handle PHI on their behalf. A BAA is the contract that binds a vendor to HIPAA’s safeguards. One thing that surprises students: health data isn’t automatically PHI. A consumer fitness app that isn’t working for a provider or health plan generally falls outside HIPAA, though other laws may apply.  SOC 2 is an independent attestation under the AICPA’s Trust Services Criteria. Two details matter. First, scope: the report covers only the systems and criteria the company chose to include, so read the scope section before trusting the logo. Second, type: a Type I report says the controls are designed properly at a single point in time. A Type II report says they operated effectively over a period, usually 6 to 12 months. Enterprise buyers want Type II.  ISO/IEC 42001 is the first international management-system standard for AI, published in 2023. It works like ISO 27001 does for security. It doesn’t tell you how to build a model. It requires the organization to have AI policies, assigned responsibilities, risk and impact assessments, and continual improvement. Unlike the NIST framework, you can be certified against it.  NIST AI RMF is voluntary U.S. guidance built around four functions. Govern sets culture and accountability. Map establishes context and identifies risks. Measure analyzes and tracks those risks. Manage prioritizes and acts on them. The Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) applies it to risks like confabulation, prompt injection, and data leakage. Nobody certifies against it, but it’s widely used as the shared vocabulary for AI risk.  Architecture implication (the callout): This is where governance becomes an engineering constraint. PHI can only go to an external service that’s covered by a BAA and has the required safeguards. In practice, that decides which LLM APIs, vector databases, logging tools, and monitoring services can sit in your pipeline. A cloud provider’s BAA covers only the services on its HIPAA-eligible list, so one non-covered service in the data path breaks compliance. That includes a third-party observability tool that captures prompts.

The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026, amended it and moved the high-risk deadlines back: Annex III systems (employment, creditworthiness, education access, essential services, law enforcement, critical infrastructure) from 2 December 2027, and Annex I (safety components of regulated products such as medical devices and machinery) from 2 August 2028. High-risk duties: conformity assessment, technical documentation, human oversight, data governance, accuracy and robustness, logging.  Prohibitions have applied since 2 February 2025: social scoring, manipulation or exploitation of vulnerabilities causing significant harm, untargeted facial scraping, emotion recognition at work and school, and real-time remote biometric identification for law enforcement with narrow exceptions. The Omnibus added systems that generate non-consensual intimate imagery or CSAM.  Article 50 transparency did arrive on 2 August 2026, so it is in force now. Chatbots must disclose they are AI; providers must mark synthetic content in machine-readable form (with a grace period to 2 December 2026 for systems already on the market); deployers disclose deepfakes and AI-generated public-interest text. Any customer-facing chatbot shipping today, including a Track C agent, must disclose that it is an AI.  General-purpose AI models, the foundation models behind Bedrock, have had their own obligations since 2 August 2025. Building an application on one generally makes you a provider or deployer of an AI system, not a GPAI model provider; substantially modifying a model can shift some obligations.  US state law: there is no comprehensive federal privacy or AI law. California's CCPA automated decisionmaking regulations require pre-use notices, opt-out or appeal rights and risk assessments for significant decisions (lending, housing, education, employment, healthcare), with compliance required from 1 January 2027. Colorado SB 26-189 repeals and replaces the 2024 Colorado AI Act and regulates automated decisionmaking in consequential decisions from 1 January 2027. Several other states grant a right to opt out of profiling. Map the states you serve at design time and revisit at each release.  The practical posture is on the bottom line: the extended deadlines are time to build the controls, not a reason to defer them.  Transition: Now you apply it. ## Slide 34
- GDPR for AI Builders: Core Obligations
> Notes: When GDPR applies to your AI system, what does it actually require you to build?  Start with scope, because students often get it wrong. GDPR doesn’t follow citizenship or residency. It applies in two cases. The first is when you process data in the context of an EU establishment, wherever the processing physically happens. The second is when you’re outside the EU but offer goods or services to people in the EU, or monitor their behavior there. A U.S. startup whose recommendation model tracks users in Germany is in scope. An EU citizen using a U.S. service while living in Utah generally is not.  1. Lawful basis + ePrivacy. Every purpose needs its own lawful basis: consent, contract, legitimate interest, and so on. “Training a model” is a separate purpose from “providing the service.” Cookies and similar tracking fall under a separate law, the ePrivacy rules, and non-essential tracking generally needs prior consent. Strictly necessary cookies are exempt. Build: a record of purposes, consent controls, and a way to withdraw consent that’s as easy as giving it.  2. Objections to direct marketing. Most GDPR rights involve balancing. This one doesn’t. If someone objects to direct marketing, including profiling done for marketing, you stop. There’s no legitimate-interest override. Build: a suppression flag that every marketing pipeline and model-scoring job actually checks, not just the email tool.  3. Automated decisions, Article 22. This is the one that hits AI systems hardest. Decisions made solely by automation that produce legal or similarly significant effects, like credit, hiring, or insurance, are restricted. The exceptions are contract necessity, authorization by law, or explicit consent. Even under an exception, people are entitled to safeguards. Build: meaningful human review, explanations of the logic involved, and a way to contest decisions. “Meaningful” is the key word: a human who rubber-stamps the model’s output doesn’t take the decision out of “solely automated.”  4. Individual rights. Access, rectification, erasure, restriction, objection, and portability. Each has its own conditions and exceptions, so erasure isn’t absolute and portability applies only in some cases. Build: a request workflow backed by data lineage. Here’s a question to ask the class: if someone requests erasure, can you find every copy of their data, including feature stores, training snapshots, and backups? Without lineage, you can’t honor the right, and you can’t prove you did.
