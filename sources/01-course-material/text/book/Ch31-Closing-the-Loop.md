31.1 Motivation
Consider a representative scenario: a large insurance company has run AI for three years across
seven production systems. Each system has its own team. Each team has its own metrics. Each
quarter, results are reported to the respective business units. Nobody aggregates the lessons
learned or asks what the portfolio of operational experience implies for the next round of AI
investments.
Then a consultant runs a retrospective. The ﬁndings are striking. Four of the seven systems de-
livered less value than projected because of the same root cause: poor data quality in a shared
upstream system that every team had worked around independently. Two systems discovered
the same customer behavior insight, but neither product team acted on it. One system had been
running at a negative ROI for eight months without triggering a portfolio review. Three years of
operational learning, and almost none of it had fed back into strategy, product roadmaps, or plat-
form decisions. Each generation of AI systems started mostly from scratch, repeating expensive
discoveries the prior generation had already made. This is the feedback failure.
The evidence that it is the dominant differentiator between adaptive and stalled AI enterprises is
now quantitative. McKinsey’s 2025 StateofAI survey reports that the vast majority of organizations
now use AI in at least one function, but only a small fraction qualify as “AI high performers”
by ﬁnancial impact. That ﬁnancial impact tracks less with better models or larger budgets than
with workﬂow redesign coupled with management practices that institutionalize learning from
operational experience (Singla et al. 2025). Two decades earlier, the DORA program reached an
analogous conclusion for software delivery: the practices that separate elite from low performers
come down to the cadence and quality of feedback loops, not tooling choices (Forsgren et al. 2018).
The pattern applies to AI portfolios as well.
Operational experience is the most valuable input into AI strategy. It is more grounded than mar-
ket research, more honest than internal advocacy, and, unlike most analyst reports, speciﬁc enough
to act on. When that experience does not feed back, the enterprise operates strategically blind,

Operational intelligence is the evidence generated by AI systems in production: technical teleme-
try, user behavior, business outcomes, incidents, cost data, governance ﬁndings, and human feed-
back. This chapter deﬁnes how that intelligence becomes enterprise learning.
31.2 Challenge
How do you build the organizational mechanisms that systematically capture operational learn-
ings from deployed AI systems and route them to the people and processes that make strategy,
product, and platform decisions, so that each generation of AI builds on the last rather than starting
over? Stated more concretely: how do you turn the dashboards, postmortems, value scorecards,
governance ﬁndings, and monitoring telemetry produced by the prior chapters of Part 4 into a
closed loop that changes what the enterprise funds, builds, and ships?
31.3 Objectives
By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
1. Identify the four categories of operational signals that should feed back into enterprise
decision-making, and the decision makers each signal must reach.
2. Design feedback mechanisms that route the right signal to the right decision maker at the
right cadence, anchored on the metric pyramid established in Chapter 26.

3. Connect operational learnings to strategy reviews, product roadmap updates, platform im-
provements, and governance reﬁnements without losing ﬁdelity at handoff.
4. Distinguish tactical feedback loops (fast, local, system-level) from strategic feedback loops
(slow, cross-functional, portfolio-level), and recognize the failure mode of each in isolation.
5. Build the organizational habits, review structures, and cultural infrastructure that make con-

8. Design an AWS-based architecture for capturing, storing, routing, and acting on operational

31.4 Interface With the Prior Chapter: The Metric Pyramid as Routing
Infrastructure
The chapter on Metrics, Benchmarks, and Guardrails (Chapter 26) introduced a four-layer metric
pyramid: business outcomes at the top, user experience and adoption beneath, model outputs
in production below that, and the model-plus-system technical ﬂoor at the bottom. Measuring
Business Value (Chapter 30) then used it as an attribution discipline, verifying that improvements
propagate from the base to the apex. In this chapter, the same pyramid operates as routing infras-
tructure.
Each layer of the pyramid speaks to a different decision-maker class and a different cadence. Engi-
neering teams live at the bottom two layers and operate on hourly or daily cycles. Product teams
own the user-experience layer and operate on weekly to monthly cycles. Portfolio leaders, ﬁnance
partners, and the CAIO consume the business outcomes layer and operate on quarterly to annual
cycles. The pyramid sorts signals by who needs them and how quickly they need to act on them,
doing more than simply measuring. Routing failure occurs when the signal cannot traverse the
pyramid: when model drift is invisible to product, or when business outcomes never make their
way back down to engineering as a retraining priority.
Airbnb’s Minerva platform is a useful example of this routing discipline. Minerva enforces a single
source of truth for every business and product metric and exposes the same deﬁnitions to execu-
tives, product managers, and engineers, so that what appears in an executive dashboard is the
same number an engineer sees in a debug tool (Amit Pahwa et al. 2021). The technology matters
less than the discipline behind it: the pyramid routes signals reliably only when every layer reads
from the same deﬁnitions. Without that, each layer interprets a different reality, and the feedback
loop closes on noise instead of evidence.

31.5 Core Concepts: Signals, Metrics, Findings, and Decisions
Before examining the four signal categories, it helps to distinguish four related concepts that prac-
titioners often conﬂate.
A metric is a measured quantity: latency, conversion rate, cost per inference, or model accuracy on
a holdout set. Metrics are the raw material of operational intelligence.
A signal is an interpreted pattern that warrants attention. A single latency spike is noise; a sus-
tained increase in latency across peak hours is a signal. Signal interpretation requires context:
baselines, thresholds, and domain knowledge.
A ﬁnding is a validated explanation of the signal. The latency signal might trace back to a database
connection pool exhaustion, an increase in model size, or an upstream service degradation. Find-
ings require investigation and often cross-functional input.
A decision is a funded or assigned change based on the ﬁnding: scale the connection pool, revert
the model, or escalate to the upstream team. Decisions require owners, timelines, and recorded
rationale.
Operational intelligence becomes enterprise learning only when all four steps are complete. Most
organizations capture metrics. Fewer interpret signals reliably. Fewer still validate ﬁndings with
rigor. Many never record decisions in a way that creates an auditable learning trail. The discipline
of this chapter is to close all four steps at the right cadence for each signal category.

31.6 The Four Categories of Operational Signal
Not all operational experience is equally useful as a strategic input. The signals that deserve to
travel upward and outward fall into four categories, each routing to a different set of decision-
makers and operating on a different cadence.
31.6.1 Performance and Value Signals
These signals describe which AI systems are delivering on their value hypotheses, which are under-
performing, and where value is being created in unexpected areas the original investment thesis
did not anticipate. They also surface the largest gaps between technical performance and business
outcomes, which signal either an attribution problem (the model works, but the business cannot
see it) or a product-market ﬁt problem (the model works, but customers do not want what it pro-
duces).
Performance and value signals route to AI portfolio strategy, product investment prioritization,
and executive reporting. They are the dominant input into the quarterly portfolio review described
later in this chapter.
A subtler performance signal is what Johnson and colleagues call slice-discovery feedback: aggre-
gate value metrics that appear healthy at the portfolio level often mask subgroup underperfor-
mance, in which one customer segment, geography, or product line is being meaningfully harmed
by an AI system whose mean performance is acceptable (Johnson et al. 2023). The strategy re-
view needs to see those slices; otherwise it makes investment decisions on averages that conceal
localized failures.

31.6.2 Data and Platform Signals
These signals describe which data gaps are limiting model quality across multiple systems, which
platform capabilities are bottlenecks causing repeated engineering effort or delays, and which
shared infrastructure investments would beneﬁt the most systems. They route to data strategy
(see Chapter 8), platform roadmap decisions, and engineering prioritization.
Data and platform signals matter disproportionately because they compound. A data gap that
affects one system is a system problem. A data gap that affects four systems is a strategic problem,
and ﬁxing it once unlocks every future AI investment that touches the affected data domain. The
platform pattern is the same: every team rebuilding the same feature engineering logic, the same
prompt versioning, or the same evaluation harness is a structural signal of a missing platform
capability.
A related signal is the accumulation of AI technical debt. When the same incidents recur across
systems, when the same workarounds proliferate, when the same lifecycle gates get skipped under
deadline pressure, the signal points to accumulated structural weakness in the lifecycle process,
not a series of one-off bugs. Breck and colleagues at Google formalized this as the ML test score, a
production-readiness gate that any system must clear before it is allowed to operate at scale (Breck
et al. 2017). Recurring test-score failures across the portfolio are a platform signal as much as a
process one.
31.6.3 Governance and Risk Signals
These signals describe which governance processes are creating friction without proportionate risk
reduction, which risk categories materialized in production that were not anticipated in the gover-

nance framework, and which regulatory requirements are emerging that the current governance
structure does not cover. They route to AI governance policy (Chapter 25), legal and compliance
teams, and executive risk reporting.
The NIST AI Risk Management Framework’s AI 600-1 proﬁle for generative AI deﬁnes risk cate-
gories including data privacy, value chain integration, environmental impacts, and harmful bias
(Autio et al. 2024). Most organizations track incidents but do not categorize them against an exter-
nal risk taxonomy, so the same incident class recurs without ever triggering a policy update. The
governance loop is incomplete unless operational risk surfaces are mapped back to the governance
framework on a recurring cadence.
A second governance signal is friction-to-risk ratio. When governance gates slow delivery without
measurably reducing risk, the gates are miscalibrated. The feedback signal is engineering time
spent in review queues versus risk events prevented, and the routing target is the governance
board for process redesign, not the engineering team for compliance.
31.6.4 User and Market Signals
These signals describe what user behavior in production reveals about needs, preferences, and
pain points that product design did not anticipate; where customers are using AI features in un-
expected ways, indicating underserved needs or misaligned design; and what competitive move-
ment reveals about AI capabilities the enterprise needs to build or acquire. They route to product
roadmap decisions (Chapter 13), business strategy (Chapter 4), and talent and capability planning.
User signals are the richest of the four categories and the most underused. AI systems generate
behavioral telemetry at a ﬁdelity traditional product analytics cannot match: every accepted or
rejected recommendation, every prompt revision, every fallback to a non-AI path is a labeled in-
stance of user preference revealed under realistic conditions. Treating that telemetry as product
research, beyond simple system monitoring, enables the kind of product discovery Netﬂix docu-
mented in its taste-community work, which is discussed later in this chapter.

31.8 Feedback Mechanisms: Routing Signal to Decision
Signals only create value when they reach the people who can act on them. Most organizations
have the signal: it is buried in dashboards, postmortem documents, and quarterly reviews. What
they lack is the mechanism that reliably routes it. The mechanism has two speeds.
31.8.1 Tactical Feedback Loops: Fast, Local, System-Level
Tactical loops operate at team level. They affect the current system’s operation, conﬁguration, or
near-term roadmap. The cadence is weekly or sprint-based, and the owner is the AI Product Owner
together with the engineering team. Representative tactical loops include:
• A monitoring alert that triggers a retraining decision, governed by the model lifecycle process
from Chapter 27.
• An A/B test result that drives a model version promotion or rollback. Kohavi and colleagues
at Microsoft document that the value of an experimentation platform lies in routinely making
promotion decisions on test results, with rollback as a ﬁrst-class outcome, rather than simply
running tests (Kohavi et al. 2020).
• A multi-armed bandit arm allocation that adjusts trafﬁc to higher-performing variants in
response to live production signal (Sweet 2023).
• A user behavior signal that drives a prompt or feature update in an LLM-based system.
Aryan’s LLMOps treatment emphasizes that prompt and tool-call telemetry must ﬂow back
to the prompt registry and eval suite on the same cadence as code, or the system silently
regresses (Aryan 2025).
8

<!-- page 9 -->
• An eval-drift observation in production that drives an update to the eval suite itself. Grace
and colleagues at Anthropic argue that evals are themselves living artifacts that must be
revised based on production signal, not static contracts written once (Grace et al. 2026).
• An incident postmortem that drives a runbook update, monitoring threshold change, or a
new pre-production check, anchored on Site Reliability Engineering practice (Betsy Beyer,
Chris Jones, Jennifer Petoff, and Niall Richard Murphy 2016).
• An error-budget breach that triggers a reliability prioritization decision, following the SLO-
driven model that Thurgood and colleagues document (Thurgood et al. 2018).
The risk of tactical loops in isolation is local optimization. They can drift away from strategic intent
if not periodically synchronized. Each loop closed quickly may improve its local metric while the
portfolio drifts away from its top-line objective. Saltonstall and Virji’s guidance on ML SLIs argues
for the same coupling: tactical SLI changes must roll up to portfolio SLOs so that local optimization
is visible at the portfolio level (Saltonstall and Virji 2025).
31.8.2 Strategic Feedback Loops: Slow, Cross-Functional, Portfolio-Level
Strategic loops operate at portfolio level. They affect investment decisions, roadmaps, platform
priorities, and governance policy. The cadence is quarterly or annual, and the owners are the CAIO,
product leadership, the AI Governance Board, and ﬁnance partners. Representative strategic loops
include:
• A value scorecard review that drives portfolio investment reallocation. Storment and Fuller’s
FinOps treatment frames this as continuous portfolio rebalancing rather than annual budget
setting (J.R. Storment and Mike Fuller 2023).
• A cross-system data gap pattern that drives a data strategy revision and a CDO-sponsored
remediation project.
• A governance ﬁnding repeated across multiple systems that drives a policy update rather
than another system-level ﬁx.
• A market signal observable only from production behavior that informs the strategic
roadmap.
• A Gartner-style ROI assessment of generative AI investments that drives a scaling-or-
sunsetting decision at the portfolio level (Gartner 2025).
• An annual build-versus-buy review for foundation models and LLM infrastructure (Ruzga
2026).
The risk of strategic loops in isolation is staleness. Annual strategy reviews without operational
grounding produce plans disconnected from reality. Both loops are required, and neither substi-
tutes for the other.
31.8.3 The Cadence Architecture
The two loop speeds resolve into a four-tier review cadence. Each tier feeds the next, and each
has explicit inputs, outputs, and owners. The architecture parallels the high-performer practices
documented in DORA’s software delivery, where deployment frequency, lead time for change,
time to restore service, and change failure rate each correspond to a feedback cadence rather than
a tool (Forsgren et al. 2018). The FinOps Foundation’s AI capability set provides useful vocabulary
for the portfolio tier (Markanastasakis et al. 2025).

31.9 AWS Reference Architecture for Operational Intelligence
On AWS, the operational intelligence pipeline that feeds the cadence architecture can be imple-
mented with the following components:
Telemetry Collection. Amazon CloudWatch captures metrics, logs, and traces from all AI work-
loads. Evidently AI detects data drift, model quality degradation, and bias drift for deployed
endpoints. Bedrock provides usage logs and, where conﬁgured, evaluation results and guardrail
violations. Application-level telemetry ﬂows through CloudWatch Logs or Amazon Kinesis Data
Streams for real-time processing.
Event Routing. Amazon EventBridge routes operational events to appropriate consumers. A drift
detection event from Evidently AI can trigger a Step Functions workﬂow for automated retraining,
a notiﬁcation to the engineering team via SNS, and an entry in the decision log. EventBridge rules
classify events by severity and signal category, enabling different routing paths for tactical versus
12

<!-- page 13 -->
strategic signals.
Storage and Cataloging. Raw operational data lands in Amazon S3, organized by date, system,
and signal category. AWS Glue Data Catalog provides schema management and enables cross-
system queries. Amazon DataZone can provide governed access to operational intelligence data
for cross-functional teams.
Data Quality and Governance. AWS Glue Data Quality or the open-source Deequ library validates
data pipelines and surfaces quality issues before they become model failures. AWS Lake Formation
enforces access controls so that sensitive operational data (such as user behavior telemetry subject
to privacy constraints) is appropriately restricted.
Analysis and Dashboarding. Amazon Athena enables ad-hoc queries across operational data in
S3. Amazon Redshift provides the analytical layer for complex cross-system analysis. Amazon
QuickSight delivers executive dashboards for portfolio review and operational dashboards for en-
gineering teams. The same metric deﬁnitions must appear in both tiers, following the Minerva
principle.
Model and Prompt Lifecycle. SageMaker Model Registry tracks model versions, training meta-
data, and approval status. SageMaker Model Cards document model behavior, limitations, and
evaluation results. For LLM workloads, Bedrock Prompt Management (or a custom prompt reg-
istry in DynamoDB) versions prompts and tracks which prompt versions are in production.
Incident and Action Tracking. AWS Systems Manager OpsCenter or integration with Servi-
ceNow/Jira tracks incidents, postmortem actions, and decision follow-ups. Each postmortem
creates an OpsItem with linked ﬁndings, decisions, and owners.
Compliance and Audit. AWS CloudTrail logs all API activity. AWS Conﬁg tracks resource conﬁg-
uration changes. AWS Security Hub aggregates security ﬁndings. AWS Audit Manager provides
evidence collection for compliance frameworks. These services ensure that operational intelligence
decisions are auditable and that governance ﬁndings surface to the appropriate review tier.
Cost Intelligence. AWS Cost and Usage Reports, Cost Explorer, and Cost Anomaly Detection
provide the cost denominator for ROI calculations. Tagging AI workloads by system, team, and
business unit enables portfolio-level cost attribution.
The infrastructure can be provisioned and version-controlled with Terraform as the standard IaC
tool, or with AWS CDK for teams that prefer infrastructure as TypeScript or Python.

31.10 Retail Customer Acquisition and Retention Use Case: Closing the
Operational-to-Strategic Loop
NorthStar operates three core AI systems: a recommendation engine that personalizes product dis-
covery, a churn prediction model that identiﬁes at-risk customers for retention interventions, and
an LLM-based offer generation system that creates personalized marketing messages. After eigh-
teen months in production, the AI portfolio team recognizes that each system generates valuable
operational intelligence, but that intelligence rarely reaches the people who set strategy, prioritize
product features, or invest in platform capabilities. The feedback loop is open.
Performance and Value Signals. The recommendation engine delivers measurable lift in average
order value, but the churn prediction model shows declining precision in one customer segment:
customers acquired through a recent partnership channel. The value scorecard reveals the aggre-
gate ROI of the AI portfolio is healthy, but slice-discovery analysis surfaces that the partnership-
acquired segment is experiencing a 12% higher churn rate than the model predicted. This signal,
invisible in portfolio averages, becomes apparent in the quarterly portfolio review and triggers a
14

<!-- page 15 -->
retraining decision using segment-speciﬁc features.
Data and Platform Signals. Two teams independently discover the same data gap: real-time in-
ventory availability is inaccessible to both the recommendation engine andar the offer generation
system. Both systems recommend or promote products that turn out to be out of stock, damaging
customer trust. When the monthly product review aggregates this ﬁnding across systems, it be-
comes a platform signal rather than a system bug. The CDO sponsors a data project to expose the
inventory state through a shared feature store, unlocking both systems and any future AI invest-
ment that touches product availability.
Governance and Risk Signals. The LLM-based offer generation system triggers a guardrail viola-
tion when it generates a promotional message that inadvertently references a competitor by name.
The incident is minor, but the governance board reviews the guardrail conﬁguration and discovers
the violation pattern has occurred three times across different campaigns. Rather than adding an-
other ad-hoc ﬁlter, the governance board updates the policy to require competitor-mention checks
as a standard guardrail category for all marketing-facing LLM outputs. The policy artifact has
been updated; the model card for the offer generation system has been revised to document the
new constraint.
User and Market Signals. Application telemetry from the recommendation engine reveals an un-
expected pattern: customers who dismiss three or more recommendations in a single session sub-
sequently convert at a higher rate than customers who accept the ﬁrst recommendation. Product
investigation reveals that these customers are using the recommendation dismissal as a ﬁltering
mechanism rather than a rejection signal. The product team revises the recommendation UI to
make explicit ﬁltering easier, informed by production behavior the original design did not antici-
pate. This signal routes from the monthly product review to the feature backlog with high priority.
Cadence Architecture in Action. Weekly system operational reviews catch the inventory latency
issue and surface it as a tactical adjustment. Monthly product performance reviews aggregate
segment-level churn drift and user-ﬁltering behavior. The quarterly portfolio review receives the
data gap ﬁnding as a platform investment request and the governance violation pattern as a policy
update proposal. The annual strategy review receives a consolidated view: the AI portfolio is
delivering value, but the biggest constraint on the next wave of investments is data accessibility,
not model capability. The CAIO synthesizes this into a recommendation to shift 15% of the next
ﬁscal year’s AI investment from new model development to data infrastructure.
The feedback loop is now closed. Each operational insight reaches the decision-maker who can

31.11 Key Takeaways
• Operational experience is the most valuable input into AI strategy, more grounded than mar-
ket research and more honest than internal advocacy; organizations that institutionalize oper-
ational feedback loops achieve disproportionate ﬁnancial impact from AI investments (Singla
et al. 2025).
• The metric pyramid from Chapter 26 serves as routing infrastructure, sorting signals by who
needs them and how quickly they must act; without consistent metric deﬁnitions across lay-
ers, feedback loops close on noise rather than evidence (Amit Pahwa et al. 2021).

• Four signal categories require distinct routing: performance and value signals to portfolio

31.12 Apply It
1. Map your current feedback paths. For one production AI system in your organization, trace
what happens when a monitoring alert ﬁres. Document each handoff: who receives the alert,
who investigates, who decides on action, and where the decision is recorded. Identify where
signal is lost or delayed, and propose one process change to close the gap.
2. Audit your signal routing against the four categories. Using the signal routing matrix
(tbl. 31.1), classify the last ten operational ﬁndings from your AI portfolio by signal category.
For each, identify whether it reached the appropriate owner and review tier. Calculate the
percentage that reached the correct decision maker within the appropriate cadence.
3. Design a portfolio review agenda. Draft a quarterly AI portfolio review agenda for your
organization. Include required inputs (value scorecards, cost trends, governance ﬁndings,
platform requests), expected outputs (investment reallocation decisions, policy updates, plat-
form priorities), and a RACI for each agenda item. Specify who presents each input and who
owns each output decision.
4. Conduct a slice-discovery analysis. For one AI system that reports healthy aggregate per-
formance, identify three subgroups (customer segments, geographies, product lines, or time
windows) and calculate performance metrics for each slice. Document any slice where per-
formance is materially worse than the aggregate, and propose how that ﬁnding should route
to the appropriate review tier.
